# THE SOVEREIGN FORTRESS: FULL ORGANIZATIONAL ARCHITECTURE
### Four Pillars, Eight Ministries, and the Complete Chain of Command
*A structural design document — grounded in Haiti's real administrative geography (10 départements, 42 arrondissements, 149 communes (sources range 140–149), ~570 communal sections) and real existing ministry names, so the transition is recognizable rather than invented from nothing.*

---

## HOW THIS DOCUMENT DIFFERS FROM THE EARLIER DRAFTS

Two corrections carried over from the design review, applied throughout:

1. **A Fourth Pillar (independent judiciary) is added.** No branch — not the Assembly, not the Executive Council, not the civil service — can detain, fine, seize assets, or silence press without a court that answers to neither of them. This is the single change that determines whether the rest of this document is a democracy or isn't.
2. **Punitive/family-control mechanisms from earlier drafts are removed**, not softened: no forced DNA testing, no abolition of legal defense, no extrajudicial tribunals, no unilateral asset seizure by an internal security directorate. Where those drafts solved a real problem (child abandonment, unequal legal defense, corruption), the fix below solves the same problem through due process instead.
3. **Parts 37–47 (October 2026 revision)** add the Dessalines Charter, a map of the 1987 Constitution and its reform proposals, a shield against private-sector capture of officials between selection and swearing-in, a child-protection and orphanage framework, armed-group judgment and reintegration, skills prisons, a technology hub, rules against foreign command of the public force, a strict border and a "cost of disrespect" doctrine, a full corruption and foreign-influence review, and a public comment-and-vote process (Part 47). Where a new Part conflicts with an older one (18.3, 19, 29.1, 33.2, 33.5, 35.2, 35.4), the newer Part governs and the older one carries a pointer.

  │ (7, rotating Pres.)│  │  JUDICIARY          │  │  SERVICE (NASA)     │

## PART 2 — THE INDEPENDENT JUDICIARY (THE FOURTH PILLAR)

This is new relative to earlier drafts and is the load-bearing fix. Designed against a specific failure mode: a court captured *once* by concentrated wealth, for life, is a single point of failure (this is the structural critique of the U.S. Supreme Court model). The fix is to make capture require compromising several independently-timed, independently-selected bodies at once — not one nomination fight.

### 2.1 Structure

| Body | Composition | Term | Selection |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tribinal Konstitisyonèl** (Constitutional Court) | 9 judges | 12 years, non-renewable, staggered (1 seat turns over roughly every 16 months) | Judicial Nominating Council proposes 3 names per vacancy → Citizens' Assembly confirms by **two-thirds** supermajority |
| **Tribinal Kasasyon** (Court of Cassation — final court of ordinary appeal, existing institution retained) | Career judges | Standard judicial career term | Promoted through career judiciary (below), not political appointment |
| **Tribinal Administratif ak Ekonomik** (Administrative & Economic Court) | Career judges + 2 sortition citizen-assessors per panel | Standard career term / 1-year rotation for citizen-assessors | Career judiciary + lottery from eligible-citizen pool |
| **Regional Courts of Appeal** (one per department, existing institution retained) | Career judges | Standard career term | Career judiciary |
| **Court of First Instance** (one per arrondissement) | Career judges | Standard career term | Career judiciary |
| **Justice of the Peace** (one per commune, existing institution reformed) | Career judge + rotating 5-citizen civic panel for minor disputes | 1-year civic panel rotation | Career judiciary + sortition |

### 2.2 The Judicial Nominating Council

A separate body from the Assembly, so no single vote ever fully controls the bench:
- 5 senior career jurists (elected by secret peer ballot of the judiciary itself)
- 5 citizens (sortition-selected, 2-year term, same eligibility pool as the Assembly)
- 2 law faculty representatives (elected by the bar/law schools)

It proposes; the Assembly confirms by supermajority. Two independently-selected bodies must agree, at different times, for any seat to be filled.

### 2.3 Career Judiciary (the bulk of the system)

Following the French/German model: entry via the NASA Judicial Exam track (see Part 4), promotion by peer review and case-outcome audit — not political appointment. This means only the small Constitutional Court at the apex requires the nomination process above; the working judiciary is insulated from politics entirely by being a civil-service career, not an appointed one.

### 2.4 Removal

A judge can only be removed by: (a) a documented finding of misconduct from the independent **Komisyon Etik Jidisyè** (Judicial Ethics Commission, cross-selected: peer jurists + sortition citizens + law faculty representatives, per Part 25 fix #3 — not judges alone, and not executive-selected), **and** (b) a two-thirds Assembly vote. Neither the Executive Council nor a simple Assembly majority can remove a judge alone. This prevents both capture (judges can't be threatened by an easy removal) and judicial dictatorship (judges aren't unaccountable either).

### 2.5 Case assignment

All cases are assigned by verified random draw, logged on the same public digital ledger used for the tax system — this eliminates "judge shopping" by wealthy litigants, which is one of the main channels through which money currently buys favorable rulings in weak judiciaries.

### 2.6 The National Public Defender Service (replacing the earlier "ban private defense" idea)

The goal in the earlier draft — a billionaire and a farmer getting equal-quality defense — is achievable without abolishing due process:
- **Sèvis Defandè Piblik Nasyonal**: a fully funded, NASA-exam-staffed public defender corps, paid at the same salary grade as prosecutors (removing the historic imbalance where prosecutors are well-funded and public defenders are not).
- Anyone may still hire private counsel — that right isn't revoked — but the public option must be genuinely excellent, not a token, so it is the rational choice for almost everyone.
- Defendant retains full appeal rights through the ordinary court structure above.

### 2.7 Judicial budget protection

The judiciary's budget is fixed as a constitutionally guaranteed minimum share of the national budget (a mechanism used in Guatemala and Costa Rica), so no other branch can defund the courts into submission as a backdoor control method.

### 2.8 What this means for the DSI (anti-corruption directorate) and the Border Tactical Shield

Both bodies from earlier drafts are kept, but with one binding change: **neither may detain a person or freeze/seize assets on its own authority.** Both can investigate and *refer* cases; the Tribinal Administratif ak Ekonomik must issue a warrant or order before any freeze, seizure, or detention proceeds. The Tactical Shield's internal deployment additionally requires a judicial emergency-powers review, not an Assembly vote alone, and carries an automatic sunset clause requiring renewal.

## PART 3 — TERRITORIAL SCAFFOLDING (grounded in Haiti's real geography)

The civil service and judiciary both run on the same five-level territorial ladder, which already exists in Haiti and does not need to be invented:

```
NATION
  └── 10 DÉPARTEMENTS (Ouest, Nord, Sud, Artibonite, Nord-Est, Centre,
                        Grand'Anse, Nippes, Nord-Ouest, Sud-Est)
        └── 42 ARRONDISSEMENTS
              └── 149 COMMUNES (sources range 140–149)
                    └── ~570 SECTIONS COMMUNALES (smallest existing unit)
```

Every Super-Ministry below deploys staff at all five levels, using the same title tiers (Part 4), so a Haitian citizen encountering "the state" always understands the rank of the person in front of them regardless of which ministry they represent.

## PART 4 — THE UNIVERSAL CIVIL SERVICE RANK LADDER

Applies inside each of the 8 Super-Ministries below. Titles are given in Kreyòl (working language of administration) with French/English equivalents, since Kreyòl is the constitutionally primary administrative language in this design.

| Tier | Title (Kreyòl) | Equivalent | Level | Selected By | Term |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Minis** | Head Minister | National | NASA shortlist of 3 → public interview → Citizens' Assembly vote | 5 years |
| 2 | **Sekretè Jeneral** | Secretary-General | National | Career promotion within ministry | Permanent civil servant (provides continuity when Minister's term ends) |
| 3 | **Direktè Jeneral** | Director-General (one per major function, see Part 5) | National | NASA senior-track promotion, peer-reviewed | Permanent, performance-audited |
| 4 | **Direktè Depatmantal** | Departmental Director | 1 per department (10 total) | NASA exam + departmental rotation requirement | 4 years, renewable once |
| 5 | **Kowòdonatè Awondisman** | Arrondissement Coordinator | 1 per arrondissement (42 total) | NASA exam, mid-career track | 4 years |
| 6 | **Enjenyè/Ofisye Kominal** | Commune Officer/Engineer | 1 per commune (149 today) | NASA entry-to-mid exam track | 4 years, answers directly to that commune's local Communal Assembly |
| 7 | **Ajan Seksyon Kominal** | Communal Section Agent | 1+ per communal section (~570 total) | NASA entry-level exam | 2 years, front-line service delivery |

This ladder is identical in shape to how the existing Haitian state is already organized administratively (departmental directors and arrondissement/commune structures already exist) — the change is *how people get the job* (exam, not patronage) and *who they answer to* (the local Communal Assembly at tiers 6–7, not a party boss).

## PART 6 — PRESS, INTERNET & DIGITAL RIGHTS (institutional design, not a censorship code)

Earlier drafts flagged that "the body of experts will do these things" — but a press/internet *law* is exactly the kind of thing that must never be drafted by a single body with an interest in controlling its own coverage. Here is the institutional shape; the actual statutory text should be drafted by the Ministry of Justice's legislative drafting office and must clear constitutional review by the Tribinal Konstitisyonèl before the Assembly can vote on it — no branch writes press law about itself unchecked.

**Enstiti Endepandan pou Laprès ak Dwa Dijital** (Independent Press & Digital Rights Commission)
- Board composition: sortition citizens + elected press-association representatives + law faculty — mirrors the Judicial Nominating Council's cross-check logic so no single interest controls media rules.
- **Guaranteed by constitutional principle, not ministry decree:** no prior restraint on publication; defamation is a civil matter resolved in ordinary courts, never an administrative penalty; journalist source-shield protections; media-ownership caps to prevent a single oligarch or family from owning the country's information supply (same logic as antitrust, applied to media).
- **Internet governance:** net neutrality requirement; a narrowly defined cybercrime statute (fraud, child exploitation material, direct incitement to imminent violence) adjudicated by the ordinary courts — never by executive administrative order; no government internet shutdown power without both a judicial emergency finding and Assembly ratification, with automatic sunset.
- **Data privacy:** citizen data rights modeled on established frameworks (right to access, correct, and delete personal data held by state or private entities), enforced by the same Commission with appeal to the Tribinal Administratif ak Ekonomik.

## PART 7 — COMPENSATION & FINANCE (a grade system, not invented salary figures)

Rather than fabricate currency amounts that would be stale the moment they're written, compensation runs on a **National Civil Service Grade Scale**, set annually by the same Nordic-style tripartite body (Government + Workers' Union + Employers' Association) that sets private-sector wage floors — this keeps public and private compensation from diverging unfairly in either direction.

| Grade | Applies to | Multiple of national median formal wage (indicative, reset annually) |
|---|---|---|
| A | Minis, Direktè Jeneral, Constitutional Court judges | 8–10x |
| B | Sekretè Jeneral, Direktè Depatmantal, career judges, senior prosecutors/public defenders | 5–7x |
| C | Kowòdonatè Awondisman, mid-career civil service | 3–4x |
| D | Enjenyè/Ofisye Kominal | 2–3x |
| E | Ajan Seksyon Kominal, entry NASA-track | 1.5–2x |

All grades and all individual salaries are published on the open digital ledger, updated in real time — this is the actual anti-corruption mechanism, not the salary level itself.

## PART 8 — THE PUBLIC LEDGER: WHAT, HOW, WHO

### 8.1 What is logged, in real time

| Category | Content | Update trigger |
|---|---|---|
| Fiscal | Every tax return, every budget line as it's spent | Moment of transaction |
| Procurement | Contract bids, awards, payment tranches | Moment of signing/payment |
| Personnel | Every civil servant's salary grade and name (Tiers 1–7), asset disclosures for Tiers 1–4 | On appointment, annually thereafter |
| Judicial | Rulings, case-assignment random draws | On issuance |
| SEB | Farmer purchase prices and volumes, by commune | Point of purchase |
| Legislative | Assembly votes, attendance, and every draft-bill version with a visible diff against the original citizen mandate | Each drafting stage |
| Diaspora Fund | Bond issuance, allocation, project spend | Moment of transaction |

**Explicit legal carve-outs** (not administrative discretion — these are the only exemptions, kept narrow so they can't be stretched to hide corruption): individual health records, pre-charge criminal investigations, minors' identities, witness protection details, and the *operational* specifics (not the budget totals) of Border Tactical Shield deployments.

### 8.2 How — the technical architecture

- **Distributed ledger, one node per commune (149 today)**: a live copy at every Sovereign House. A change is only accepted if it matches across more than 70% of nodes — altering a record in Port-au-Prince alone does nothing if the other communes still hold the original.
- **Air-gapped separation**: treasury/currency-reserve systems are physically disconnected from the internet and only *push* signed records outward to the public ledger — they cannot be reached or altered from the public-facing side.
- **Open-source code**: both the ledger software and the sortition lottery algorithm are published in full. Any citizen, journalist, or outside academic can audit the code itself, not just its output.
- **Three access layers**, because a "public" ledger requiring a smartphone and data plan isn't actually public in Haiti's real conditions:
  1. Web portal — full detail, for anyone connected.
  2. USSD/SMS lookup — works on a basic phone with no data plan.
  3. A physical terminal at every Sovereign House, with a staffed reader available — the citizen-facing layer runs primarily in spoken and written Kreyòl, since a right that requires literacy and hardware isn't universal.
- **Individual cryptographic signatures**: every entry is signed by the specific official who executed it — not a department-wide login — so nothing passes through an anonymous "office" that could sanitize it before publication.

### 8.3 Who runs it, and who watches the people running it

- **Entry**: the civil servant who executes the transaction logs it automatically at the point of action — no separate reporting office that could edit or delay it first.
- **Verification**: the DSI and any citizen can flag anomalies (citizen flags auto-trigger a DSI review); the Cyber-Defense Directorate continuously stress-tests the system's integrity.
- **Maintenance**: the physical network is run by the Infrastructure Ministry's telecom directorate; the software is maintained by NASA-exam engineers plus open-source public contribution. Any code change altering data retention or access rules requires sign-off from the Tribinal Administratif ak Ekonomik, since that's effectively rule-making, not routine maintenance.
- **Watching the watchers**: a rotating civic panel (same model as the Justice of the Peace panels) reviews system uptime and access complaints quarterly, publicly.

## PART 9 — RESISTANCE TO FOREIGN INTERFERENCE: WHAT THIS ACTUALLY BUYS, AND WHAT IT DOESN'T

This design makes the *classic* corruption-based interference model — buy or pressure the one person who holds power — far harder than the status quo. It's worth being precise about why, and equally honest about what it leaves untouched, since overclaiming here would repeat the mistake of the earlier drafts.

### 9.1 What genuinely gets harder

- **No single bribable office.** Sortition removes the "buy the president" strategy outright — there's no one to buy before the lottery, and the person it produces has no campaign debt to anyone. The 26-day rotating figurehead removes the assassination/blackmail incentive that concentrated executive power creates.
- **No single technical target.** A hostile actor can't erase the treasury by breaching one server — they'd need to compromise more than 70% of 140 physically distributed nodes simultaneously.
- **No single court to capture.** Influencing the judiciary requires compromising multiple independently-timed, independently-selected bodies rather than one nomination fight.
- **Reduced loan-conditionality leverage.** The Zero-IMF mandate removes one of the two channels historically used to force Haitian policy changes from outside (the 1994–95 tariff reduction under IMF/US pressure being the clearest precedent).
- **A visible paper trail.** Covert payments to individual officials are harder to launder through a budget line when that line is published the day it's spent, queryable by SMS by any citizen.

### 9.2 What this design does *not* neutralize — stated plainly

- **Economic dependency is untouched by governance structure.** Diaspora remittances make up a large share of Haiti's GDP, and apparel exports and food imports create real trade dependency on the US and DR regardless of who's sitting in the Assembly. A tariff threat or a remittance-channel disruption can still pressure the country — sortition doesn't touch this exposure.
- **Information warfare targets the *voters*, not an office.** A foreign actor can't bribe a sortition-selected citizen, but can run disinformation campaigns to shape what that citizen believes before they vote. An uninformed jury is still vulnerable even when it's incorruptible.
- **Armed proxies are a security problem, not a governance-design problem.** Outside actors have historically funded and armed gangs directly; an org chart doesn't interdict weapons trafficking by itself — that needs functioning customs enforcement and sustained international cooperation on arms flows.
- **Physical infrastructure remains attackable.** The air-gapped, distributed ledger raises the bar against a *software* attack, but a well-resourced actor can still cut a cable or jam a satellite uplink rather than hack code.
- **Diplomatic isolation is a real risk of the Zero-IMF stance itself, not just a defense against it.** Rejecting the IMF/World Bank relationship can invite retaliatory isolation from those institutions and their allies. "Asymmetric Alliances" (South-South partnerships) is a reasonable hedge, but it's aspirational — other governments have their own interests, not automatic solidarity, and that relationship has to be actually built, not assumed.

**Fair summary:** this system is a strong answer to *domestic elite capture* and to the specific, well-documented historical channel of loan-conditionality and single-office bribery. It is not, and no institutional design could be, a full answer to economic dependency, information warfare, arms trafficking, or diplomatic leverage — those need their own separate strategies (trade diversification, media literacy investment, customs/interdiction capacity, coalition-building) alongside this governance structure, not instead of it.

## PART 10 — CLOSING THE FOUR REMAINING GAPS

None of these are solved by an org chart. They're solved by specific, separate mechanisms — some of which need to plug into the structure already built, and one of which requires walking back something in an earlier draft.

### 10.A Trade Dependency — Diversify, Don't Pretend to Eliminate

A small island economy cannot become self-sufficient in trade; the honest goal is reducing the damage any single partner can do, not autarky.

- **Concentration limits, tracked publicly**: the Ministry of Economy publishes a live export-concentration index on the ledger. Target: no single country above 40% of any export category within 10 years. Haiti's apparel exports are currently overwhelmingly US-bound under preferential trade programs — that concentration, not trade itself, is the vulnerability.
- **Remittance channel diversification**: formal partnerships with multiple countries' banking and mobile-money systems, not reliance on one corridor. A single country's policy change shouldn't be able to interrupt the ~20%+ of GDP remittances represent.
- **Strategic reserves**: a legally mandated minimum stockpile (months, not days) of rice and fuel held by the state — insulation against a sudden supply or price shock, cheap compared to the damage a shortage does.
- **Deepen CARICOM integration** (Haiti is already a member): regional trade as a structural hedge — a bloc has leverage a single small state doesn't.
- **Food import substitution** — already built into Part 5's agriculture program — directly shrinks the food half of this dependency.

### 10.B Disinformation Aimed at Voters, Not Officials

Worth naming a genuine strength already built into this design before adding to it: **sortition assemblies are structurally more resistant to social-media-style disinformation than mass elections**, because members receive structured expert testimony in a deliberative session, not a scrolling feed. This is a documented finding in deliberative-democracy research (Fishkin's deliberative polling work) — informed, face-to-face deliberation shifts judgment in ways an anonymous information feed doesn't. That protects the 150-member national Assembly reasonably well.

It does *not* protect: the broader public's trust in the system between Assembly cycles, or the many more numerous commune-level assemblies. For those:

- **Media literacy in the compulsory curriculum**, starting young — modeled on Finland, which trains this from primary school and is consistently ranked as Europe's most disinformation-resistant population.
- **An independent, civil-society-run fact-checking body** — modeled on Taiwan's Cofacts/g0v model — funded through the Press & Digital Rights Commission but *not* government-operated. It publishes counter-information; it does not have the power to block or remove speech. This distinction has to be absolute: the moment a body can declare something false as a matter of law and act on that declaration, it's a censorship office wearing a fact-checking label.
- **Foreign-funded political advertising disclosure**, enforced by the Commission with judicial backing (per Part 2) — visibility into who's paying for political messaging, not control over the messaging itself.

### 10.C Arms Trafficking

This needs its own mandate, separate from the food/goods tariff scanning already planned at the border checkpoints:

- **A dedicated arms-interdiction unit within Customs**, distinct from the SPS/food inspectors, at Ouanaminthe, Belladère, Malpasse, and Anse-à-Pitres.
- **Formal weapons-tracing partnership** with source-country authorities (the US ATF's eTrace program is already used cooperatively by several Caribbean nations to trace recovered firearms back to their point of sale) — this converts "guns keep showing up" into an actual paper trail leading somewhere.
- **Regional intelligence-sharing through CARICOM IMPACS** (the bloc's existing crime-and-security body) rather than trying to solve trafficking in isolation.
- **Seizure data published on the open ledger** — this does double duty: it deters seized weapons from quietly re-entering circulation through corrupt officers, and it creates public, verifiable pressure on the source countries whose export controls let the weapons out in the first place.

### 10.D Diplomatic Isolation as Retaliation for Rejecting the IMF

Here's the correction: the earlier draft's **"constitutionally illegal to ever sign an IMF/World Bank loan"** is too rigid, and rigidity itself becomes a liability — a hard "never" removes the state's own flexibility in a genuine emergency (earthquake, hurricane, pandemic) and can invite the exact retaliatory isolation it's trying to prevent.

**Better version**: any loan carrying policy conditionality requires a **two-thirds Assembly vote plus a public referendum**, rather than an absolute ban. This keeps the actual protection — no single Executive Council or minister can quietly sign the country into a structural-adjustment package — without the brittleness of a permanent constitutional prohibition that a future crisis might make the state break its own founding law to survive.

Alongside that: build the *alternative* financing relationships proactively, not just as a reaction — the Caribbean Development Bank, Inter-American Development Bank, and South-South partners should be the first-call relationships, so that raising the bar on IMF conditionality doesn't mean cutting off external finance altogether. And use CARICOM membership actively as a diplomatic shield: a coordinated regional bloc position carries weight a single small state doesn't have alone.

## PART 11 — DIPLOMATIC DOCTRINE: RESPECTFUL SOVEREIGNTY, NOT ISOLATION

The posture of courteous to every nation, deferential to none, and never conceding national interest under pressure, is worth encoding as a structural rule rather than a personality trait, for the same reason everything else in this document is structural: individual diplomats can be pressured, replaced, or bribed one at a time, but a rule that applies regardless of who's in the room can't be.

**The rule**: any Haitian diplomat, minister, or Executive Council member may engage warmly and professionally with any country's representatives — that costs nothing and buys goodwill. But **no individual can concede anything that affects sovereignty, trade terms, resource rights, or territorial matters.** Any such agreement requires the same two-thirds Assembly ratification used for major loans (Part 10.D). This means "not backing down" isn't dependent on any one person's courage in a negotiating room — the structure itself won't let a single official cave, even under direct pressure, because they don't have the authority to.

### On "no aid from any country" — the same correction pattern as the IMF fix, applied here too

Worth being just as honest about this as the IMF stance: **an absolute, unconditional refusal of all outside assistance is more dangerous than the aid-dependency problem it's meant to solve.** Haiti has faced earthquakes and hurricanes that killed and displaced enormous numbers of people within days — a blanket "no aid, ever" rule would mean refusing emergency medical teams or shelter materials in exactly the moment they save the most lives.

The real problem the "no aid" instinct is correctly reacting to isn't aid itself — it's the version of aid that has actually harmed Haiti: independent NGOs running parallel, unaccountable mini-governments; food dumping disguised as charity that destroys local farmers; assistance used as leverage to dictate policy. That's precisely what the earlier **Anti-Aid Edict** already blocks: no independent NGO operations on Haitian soil, and any assistance must be cash, routed through the treasury, logged on the open ledger, spent by an actual ministry against an actual budget line.

So the doctrine isn't "reject all aid" — it's:

- **No parallel governance.** No foreign organization operates independently inside Haiti, ever.
- **No policy-conditional aid** without the same supermajority ratification as a loan.
- **Emergency humanitarian assistance in an acute disaster** may be accepted by the Executive Council under its existing 48-hour emergency authority (Part 1), immediately logged publicly, and ratified or rejected by the Assembly once it convenes — the same emergency-then-ratify pattern already built into the Council's disaster powers, not a new exception.

This keeps the actual protection — no country buys influence by calling it "aid" — without a rigid rule that, in a real earthquake, would cost lives to make an ideological point.

## PART 12 — THE ANNUAL BUDGET CYCLE

- The Ministry of Economy's Public Treasury directorate drafts annual revenue projections (tax collection, tariffs, SEB export profits, diaspora bond proceeds) on a fixed fiscal calendar — the process cannot be indefinitely delayed by any branch.
- Each Super-Ministry submits a budget request against the priorities the Assembly has already mandated.
- The request follows the same mandate → draft → constitutional pre-check → read-back → vote sequence used for legislation (Part on legislative flow), just on an annual cycle instead of an ad hoc one.
- Every proposed line is posted to the open ledger *before* the vote, not after — so the public and press see the same draft the Assembly is voting on, in real time.
- The Tribinal Administratif ak Ekonomik can flag a revenue projection as unrealistic before the vote, the same way it reviews any other administrative action — this catches the classic trick of overpromising revenue to justify overspending.

## PART 13 — CONTINUITY ACROSS THE TWO-YEAR ASSEMBLY CYCLE

The Assembly turning over completely every two years is a deliberate anti-capture feature, but it would be a real problem for anything that takes longer than two years to build — a hospital, a rail line, a reforestation program — if nothing else in the system were designed to survive that turnover. Several things already do, and two more are added here:

- **Staggered execution terms already carry most of the weight**: Ministers serve 5 years, Directors-General are permanent and performance-audited, judges serve 12-year staggered terms. None of these reset when the Assembly does, so a project doesn't lose its institutional memory every two years even though its legislative overseers change.
- **Binding multi-year appropriations**: a capital project (rail, grid, hospital) approved by the same two-thirds supermajority used for loans and constitutional matters cannot be casually cancelled mid-build by a later Assembly — reversing it requires the same high threshold that approved it.
- **A rolling National Development Plan**: a 5–10 year horizon document, civil-service authored and Assembly-ratified, that each incoming Assembly amends incrementally rather than rewriting from zero — the same approach that let South Korea's multi-year industrial plans survive changes in government.
- **A mandatory transition briefing**: the outgoing Assembly's final session includes a formal handover from the civil service listing every active multi-year project and contractual obligation, before the incoming Assembly can vote to reverse anything. No new Assembly inherits a blank slate or gets to claim ignorance of what's already underway.

## PART 14 — FAMILY ACCOUNTABILITY: COMPLICITY, NOT GUILT BY ASSOCIATION

The governing principle, stated plainly: **punishment attaches to what a specific person did, never to who they're related to.** Two mechanisms implement the legitimate concerns behind "families shouldn't be able to hide behind each other" without collapsing into collective punishment.

### 14.1 Complicity through action — prosecuted individually, like any other crime

If a family member actively assists a crime — laundering money, hiding or moving assets, forging documents, destroying evidence, providing a false alibi — that is ordinary accessory/complicity law, already established in legal systems worldwide. It applies to that individual, tried through the independent judiciary (Part 2), with the same evidence standards and defense rights (including the National Public Defender Service) as any other defendant. This is not a special "family rule" — it's the existing complicity standard, applied without exception for anyone, family or not.

### 14.2 Mere knowledge without action — deliberately not criminalized

Most functioning democracies do not criminalize a spouse, parent, or child's silence about a relative's crime, and this is not an oversight — it's why family/spousal testimonial privilege exists as a legal concept. Making silence itself chargeable for immediate family creates a standing incentive for the state to turn relatives into forced informants against their own household — historically, this exact dynamic (recruiting family members to report on each other) has been a defining feature of surveillance states, not an anti-corruption tool. This design deliberately does not go there. Two things replace it:

- **The DSI's continuous lifestyle audits** (Part 8) watch household asset patterns directly. Investigators don't need a forced confession from a relative when unexplained wealth is already visible in the audit once it's flagged.
- **A whistleblower protection and reward track**, open to anyone — family or not — who voluntarily comes forward with evidence: legal immunity from complicity charges, plus a percentage of any recovered assets. This creates a real incentive to report without criminalizing those who don't.

### 14.3 The conflict-of-interest recusal rule — the actual answer to "family dynasties"

The legitimate concern behind wanting to sideline a corrupt official's family isn't punishment — it's preventing that family from simply continuing the same influence through a different name. That's solved directly, without touching guilt:

**A spouse, parent, child, or sibling of a currently serving or recently-removed official (within a defined cooling-off period, e.g., 4 years) may not hold a position, contract, or license that creates a direct conflict of interest with that official's former office.** This is a narrow, targeted rule about actual conflicts of interest — reviewable and appealable through the Tribinal Administratif — not a permanent civic disqualification for a crime the relative didn't commit.

## PART 15 — MONETARY RESILIENCE: CORRESPONDENT BANKING, NOT JUST SWIFT

The commonly imagined threat — a foreign government flipping a switch to cut Haiti off from SWIFT over a political dispute — is real but not the main one. The channel that has actually hurt Haiti and the wider Caribbean for over a decade is quieter: **de-risking**, where large global banks voluntarily withdraw correspondent banking relationships because the AML/CFT compliance cost of serving small, perceived-high-risk jurisdictions no longer looks profitable. A 2017 regional banking survey found the large majority of surveyed Caribbean banks had lost at least one correspondent relationship, and the pressure has not gone away — recent regional banking analysis points to tightening EU and US financial regulation as continuing to raise the cost of serving small Caribbean respondents. This reframing changes what actually helps:

- **Redundancy of relationships, not full decoupling.** No small economy can opt out of dollar-clearing trade with the US, and a fully parallel payment system nobody else agrees to use just means nobody accepts Haiti's payments. The real goal is never depending on one correspondent bank or one country's institutions for all cross-border transactions, so losing one relationship is an inconvenience, not a collapse.
- **The public ledger is also a de-risking tool.** Correspondent banks price in *perceived* money-laundering risk more than documented evidence of it. A country with a live, publicly auditable transaction ledger (Part 8) and a functioning AML/CFT framework is measurably cheaper and safer for a bank to keep serving — the transparency system built for domestic anti-corruption reasons pays a second dividend here.
- **Regional integration is the actual leverage a small state has.** CARICOM's current strategy is explicitly built around growing intra-regional trade specifically so less of it has to cross the US-dollar correspondent chain at all. Haiti plugging into that regional settlement effort matters more than a go-it-alone alternative — a bloc has negotiating weight a single country doesn't.
- **Diversify correspondent relationships across multiple countries, not just reserve currencies.** Already partly addressed via gold-backed reserves (earlier draft); the correspondent-banking side needs the same logic applied to actual banking relationships.
- **Watch emerging settlement rails, don't bet the system on them yet.** Stablecoin-based settlement and multi-central-bank digital currency pilot platforms are being discussed regionally as maturing alternatives to legacy correspondent banking, but the regulatory rules governing them are still being written. This belongs in the Data Sovereignty section (Part 7 of the earlier design) as a monitored pilot, not a load-bearing pillar.

**Honest limit**: even a perfectly transparent, corruption-free Haiti still faces some elevated cost from region-wide risk perception that no single country fully controls alone — which is exactly why the regional response matters more than a purely national one here.

## PART 16 — ASSEMBLY ONBOARDING (NOT AN ELIGIBILITY GATE)

Eligibility for the sortition pool remains deliberately uncredentialed — Haiti's secondary/baccalaureate completion rate (roughly 28% as of 2020) is heavily correlated with urban wealth and access, not civic judgment, so a bac requirement would exclude the rural majority the Assembly is specifically designed to represent, recreating by credential the exact capture problem sortition exists to eliminate.

Instead, every selected member — Citizens' Assembly and Communal Assembly alike — goes through **mandatory, paid civic onboarding after selection, before their first vote**: how the ministries work, how to read a budget, how to use the public ledger, delivered primarily in spoken Kreyòl given real literacy patterns. This follows the model actually used by Ireland's Citizens' Assembly and France's Citizens' Convention on Climate — expert briefing happens *after* the lottery, for everyone selected, not as a filter on who can be drawn. It's also consistent with the legislative flow already built into this document: experts are required to present in plain Kreyòl read-back to the Assembly, not the other way around — the system is designed to meet members where they are, not to pre-screen for people who already meet the system.

**Onboarding is also the first line of defense against capture.** It happens in a 14-day neutral Orientation Retreat with housing for members and their families provided by the state, and includes a plain walk-through of how private interests buy officials before they are sworn in (Part 39.4).

## PART 17 — EXPERT CIVIL SERVICE: ENTRY PATHWAYS

The NASA exam track answers a different question than Assembly eligibility. The Assembly is a representative jury — gating it by credential defeats its purpose. The civil service performs technical work — an engineer needs real engineering training, a judge needs real legal training — so some roles legitimately require deep preparation. The design challenge is funding that preparation rather than requiring applicants to have already paid for it themselves.

**The failure mode to design against is well documented elsewhere**: France's ENA and India's UPSC both built entrance exams intended as pure meritocracy, and both became dominated by candidates whose families could afford years of expensive private exam preparation — a "merit" filter that functioned, in practice, as a wealth filter. Haiti requiring a prior university degree just to sit the NASA exam would recreate exactly this, drawing the technocratic corps from the same narrow, historically privileged population the rest of this document is built to route around.

### 17.1 Open application, no prior credential to attempt

Any adult citizen may sit the Stage 1 Preliminary Filter (logic, mathematics, civics) with no prior degree requirement. Free state-provided preparation materials and courses are available through the commune-level Sovereign House network — the same infrastructure built for ledger access — so preparing for the exam doesn't itself require money or proximity to Port-au-Prince.

### 17.2 Two tracks, matched to what the role actually demands

| Track | Applies to | How competence is built |
|---|---|---|
| **Generalist/Entry** | Communal Section Agent, entry-level Commune Officer roles | Pass the exam, begin paid service, train on the job through state-funded part-time coursework — competence accumulates through the work itself |
| **Technical/Professional** | Engineers, doctors, judges, senior economists — Director-General level and above | Pass the aptitude exam, receive **fully state-funded professional education** (medical school, engineering, law), then serve a fixed-term service bond afterward — the same funded-training-for-service-bond model already built for the National Teaching Corps |

### 17.3 What stays constant across both tracks

Anonymized, blind grading throughout (Part 4) — this is what keeps even the funded pathway honest, ensuring the scholarship goes to whoever actually scored highest, not whoever knew the right person.

## PART 18 — BOOTSTRAPPING: FROM HERE TO THERE

Every part of this document so far describes a finished, operating system. None of it addresses how Haiti gets from its actual current situation to that system — and that gap matters more than any single institutional design choice, because a perfect constitution adopted illegitimately is worth less than a modest one adopted through a process people actually trust.

### 18.1 The real starting point, as of mid-2026

Haiti's Transitional Presidential Council concluded its mandate on February 7, 2026, without having organized elections or restored security, and handed power to an interim government under Prime Minister Alix Didier Fils-Aimé. As of October 2026 the presidency is vacant. The first round of presidential, legislative and local elections is scheduled for December 13, 2026 (it was originally set for August 30 and has already slipped once), with a runoff on February 21, 2027 and a new government expected to be inaugurated on March 12, 2027. Treat every date as likely to move again. At the same time, armed groups control significant parts of Port-au-Prince and reach into routes and towns beyond the capital, and an internationally backed Gang Suppression Force is deploying under UN Security Council authorization.

This matters for sequencing in two ways: there is already a live, internationally recognized electoral process underway, and there are parts of the country where no institution — old or new — currently has functioning reach. Neither fact is addressed by designing a better org chart.

### 18.2 What this document actually is, honestly

This is a citizen-authored proposal, not a governing text, and it shouldn't be presented as one. Its legitimate path is as **advocacy material** — something a candidate, a party, or a civil society coalition running in the 2026–2027 electoral cycle can adopt, campaign on, and bring into a legitimately elected parliament, not a document that bypasses that process. A constitution imposed without broad participatory drafting tends to fail at ratification even when its content is sound — Chile's 2019–2023 constitutional process produced two drafts that were both rejected by referendum, in part because the drafting itself didn't build broad enough ownership before the final vote. That's the lesson to build in from the start, not discover after a failed referendum.

### 18.3 The phased sequence

1. **Phase 0 (now, through the 2026–2027 electoral cycle)**: This document circulates as a public proposal. Any part of it — the sortition Assembly, the independent judiciary, the ministry consolidation — can be championed by candidates or advocated by civil society within the ongoing legitimate electoral process, not instead of it.
2. **Phase 1 (after a president and parliament are legitimately seated, 2027)**: The elected parliament — not this document, not any single author — votes to convene a **Constituent Assembly** tasked specifically with drafting a permanent constitution. Modeled on South Africa's 1994–1996 process (an elected constitution-writing body, with a constitutional court certifying the final text against agreed founding principles before it takes effect) and Tunisia's 2011–2014 National Constituent Assembly. The Constituent Assembly should mix elected delegates with a genuine cross-section of civil society, and could pilot a first, small-scale sortition-selected citizen panel within it — building public trust in the mechanism before it becomes constitutionally load-bearing.
3. **Phase 2 (drafting)**: Broad public consultation *during* drafting, not just a final up-or-down vote — the participatory step Chile's process under-invested in.
4. **Phase 3 (ratification)**: A national referendum on the finished text, with the Constitutional Court role (Part 2) not yet existing to certify it — so this one time, certification against the process's own stated founding principles falls to the Constituent Assembly itself, by supermajority, before the public vote. **Legal caution (Part 38.2):** under the 1987 Constitution a referendum may not be used to *amend* it (Art. 284-3), and amendment follows Arts. 282–284. Phase 3 is therefore available only as a *replacement* of the Constitution built on broad consensus, not as an amendment, and the recommended order is Part 38.3's three tracks: ordinary law now, advisory citizen panels next, constitutional change by the strict route last.
5. **Phase 4 (transition of personnel)**: Sitting judges, police, and civil servants are **retained provisionally**, not purged, with a defined grace window (e.g., 2 years) to individually pass through the new NASA-exam or judicial-vetting process. This avoids both a governance vacuum and the collective-punishment failure mode already rejected in Part 14 — competence and conduct are assessed person by person, not by association with the old system.
6. **Phase 5 (first sortition draw)**: Conducted under civil-society and international observation for its first cycle only, specifically to build public trust in the lottery's integrity before it operates on pure open-source verification alone.

### 18.4 The honest limit: sequencing follows security, not the other way around

A national sortition draw and a Communal Assembly in every commune cannot function in territory outside effective state presence. This document's institutional design does not substitute for the ongoing security effort — it depends on that effort succeeding first, at least regionally. The realistic rollout is **phased by territory**: communes and departments with functioning state presence stand up Communal Assemblies and civil-service posts first; contested areas join as security conditions actually change, not on a fixed calendar that assumes uniform national control that doesn't currently exist.

## PART 19 — THE CONSTITUTIONAL AMENDMENT PROCEDURE

Part 2 referenced a "constitutional amendment valve" as a check on the judiciary without specifying it. Here is the actual mechanism, in two tiers — because not every provision should be equally easy to change.

### 19.1 Entrenched clauses (the highest bar, deliberately hard to amend)

Modeled on Germany's Basic Law, which places core principles beyond ordinary amendment specifically so no future majority can vote itself out of the constraints that make it democratic: the following cannot be amended by the standard procedure below, only by an even higher bar (unanimous Executive Council + three-quarters Assembly + referendum with turnout minimum) —

- The existence and independence of the judiciary
- Sortition as the Assembly's selection method
- The prohibition on collective punishment / guilt by association (Part 14)
- Press freedom and the ban on prior restraint (Part 6)
- The universal, uncredentialed eligibility of the sortition pool (Part 16)
- The prohibition on independent domestic military policing authority (Part 33.2)
- Slavery and servitude forever abolished, including child servitude and trafficking (Dessalines Charter Art. 2; Part 37)
- The anti-capture core of Part 39: the zero-gift rule, the giver's debarment and disgorgement, the Offer Register, and the independence of the Integrity Desk
- Haitian command and Haitian pay of the public force, the contractor rules, and the ban on foreign bases without a two-thirds vote and a referendum (Part 44.5, rules 1, 4, 7, 9), and the rule that the armed force obeys civil authority and does not deliberate

These are entrenched because they're the specific mechanisms that prevent this system from being captured or converted into what it was built to replace — a future Assembly voting to weaken them is exactly the failure mode this document exists to prevent.

### 19.2 Ordinary amendments

Everything else — ministry structure, specific tariff rates, administrative details — can be amended by **two-thirds Assembly vote plus a majority public referendum.**

**Legal note (Part 38.2):** this is the design for the *finished* system. It cannot be adopted by amending the 1987 Constitution, whose Art. 284-3 forbids a referendum as an amendment method and whose Arts. 282–284 set a slower route. The route to this design is Part 38.3.

### 19.3 Who can propose

- The Assembly, by the same two-thirds threshold, or
- A **citizen petition**, modeled on Switzerland's popular-initiative system: a defined signature threshold (e.g., 5% of registered voters) forces a proposal onto the referendum ballot even without Assembly initiation — a direct-democracy safety valve against an Assembly that won't act.

### 19.4 The check on the check

The Tribinal Konstitisyonèl reviews any proposed amendment before it reaches a referendum, solely to confirm it doesn't attempt to alter an entrenched clause through the ordinary process. This is the one place the judiciary can block a vote from happening at all — deliberately narrow, and reviewable in reasoning, so it can't become a general veto over policy the court simply dislikes.

## PART 20 — LAND TENURE & THE NATIONAL CADASTRE

This is arguably the single most consequential gap for the *economic* half of this document. Haiti's land title system is widely documented as chaotic — overlapping and disputed claims, no reliable national registry, and fraud in the informal deed market. Nearly every economic mechanism already built here depends on knowing who legitimately holds which land: the SEB can't guarantee a purchase price to a farmer whose plot is under dispute, mining and tourism joint ventures can't proceed without clear title, and diaspora investment routinely stalls on exactly this problem.

### 20.1 A new directorate, not a new ministry

**DG Kadas ak Anrejistreman Tè** (Land Cadastre & Registration), added to the Ministry of Economy (Part 5.1) — land is fundamentally an economic-infrastructure function.

### 20.2 The method: systematic, not case-by-case

Modeled on Rwanda's land tenure regularization program (2000s–2010s), which formalized millions of parcels in a relatively short period using **systematic**, commune-by-commune mapping rather than waiting for individual claims to be filed one at a time — GPS/satellite survey combined with community-based adjudication, which is faster and cheaper than it would have been a generation ago.

### 20.3 Recognizing reality, not just paperwork

Many rural Haitian households have generations of de facto occupancy without formal title. A formalization process that requires proof of paperwork that never existed doesn't protect these families — it dispossesses them in favor of whoever can afford a lawyer, which would make "land reform" itself a wealth-transfer mechanism to the already-connected, the exact failure mode flagged elsewhere in this document regarding credentialed gates. **Long-term customary occupancy is recognized as a valid claim**, adjudicated locally.

### 20.4 Dispute resolution and integrity

Disputed claims go to the reformed **Civic Tribunal** (the 5-citizen local panel with state legal counsel, already built in Part 2's territorial justice design), with appeal to the Court of First Instance. The resulting registry is stored on the same 140-node public ledger (Part 8) — a land registry is just another category of public record, and using the same tamper-evident architecture blocks the classic corruption of land registries in weak states: backdating deeds, or quietly selling the same parcel's title to two different buyers.

## PART 21 — GENERAL EMERGENCY POWERS FRAMEWORK

The Executive Council's existing 48-hour authority (Part 1) is scoped narrowly to acute disasters and gang-related crises. It says nothing about a longer emergency — a pandemic, an extended blockade, a prolonged security crisis. Left undefined, that gap is exactly where "temporary" emergency powers historically become permanent — Weimar Germany's Article 48 is the textbook cautionary case: emergency decree powers, invoked repeatedly and never meaningfully time-boxed, became the mechanism through which democratic government was ultimately dismantled.

### 21.1 A second, higher tier

For crises beyond the 48-hour window:

- **Declaration**: the Executive Council declares a State of Exception, publicly, on the ledger, immediately.
- **Judicial review within 72 hours**: the Tribinal Konstitisyonèl reviews the declaration for proportionality — is the response actually matched to the threat — before it can continue.
- **Assembly ratification within two weeks**: simple majority to continue past that point.
- **Automatic 90-day sunset**: the default is expiration. Continuing past 90 days requires an affirmative two-thirds Assembly vote to renew — the burden is on justifying continuation, not on someone remembering to end it.

### 21.2 Non-derogable limits — even during a declared emergency

Modeled on the international human-rights concept of non-derogable rights (protections that cannot be suspended even during a declared emergency, found in instruments like the ICCPR): a State of Exception **cannot** be used to —

- Cancel or indefinitely postpone a scheduled sortition draw (a brief, judicially reviewed delay only, never a suspension)
- Suspend judicial review or habeas-equivalent access to a court
- Close the press or use the emergency to restrict speech beyond narrowly tailored, time-limited public-safety measures

This list exists specifically because "the emergency required it" is the most common justification history has seen for dismantling exactly these protections.

## PART 22 — CITIZENSHIP & NATURALIZATION

### 22.1 Birthright and descent

Haiti's current framework combines jus soli and jus sanguinis: birth on Haitian soil generally requires at least one Haitian parent, and descent from a Haitian parent (even born abroad) also confers citizenship. This design retains that structure.

### 22.2 Dual citizenship — explicitly protected for ordinary citizens

A 2012 constitutional amendment established dual citizenship in Haiti, though its application has reportedly been inconsistent in practice since. Given how central the diaspora is to this entire document — the Diaspora Tribunes, the Brain-Gain Fund, diaspora bonds, remittance flows — this design **explicitly and unambiguously guarantees dual citizenship for ordinary citizens**, resolving that inconsistency rather than leaving it ambiguous. It would be self-defeating to build an entire economic strategy around diaspora participation while leaving their citizenship status uncertain.

### 22.3 The one narrow exception, kept from the earlier draft

The 4-year post-service dual-citizenship/foreign-residency restriction on **diplomats specifically** (Part on the Sovereign Diplomatic Corps) stays — that's a narrow, role-specific anti-blackmail safeguard for people who negotiate on the state's behalf, not a general citizenship rule, and shouldn't be confused with one. **Part 38.5 extends the same narrow, role-based logic** to the few posts that command force, intelligence, customs, the central bank or the Constitutional Court: the holder keeps no foreign citizenship or residence while in office, because a revocable visa is leverage; every other citizen, Assembly members included, keeps dual nationality.

### 22.4 Naturalization for long-term residents

Five years of continuous legal residence, demonstrated Kreyòl proficiency, and a clean record — consistent with Haiti's existing naturalization framework, administered through the Ministry of Justice's immigration directorate with the same anonymized, auditable process used elsewhere in this document.

## PART 23 — UNIVERSAL PENSION SYSTEM

The healthcare and education guarantees in Part 5.5 have no counterpart for old age — and a large share of Haiti's workforce is informal or agricultural, meaning a purely contributory, payroll-linked pension (deducted from formal wages) would leave most citizens uncovered.

### 23.1 A two-layer design

- **A universal, non-contributory floor pension**, funded by a fixed share of state resource and export revenue (mining royalties, SEB profits) — modeled on Bolivia's *Renta Dignidad*, a resource-revenue-funded universal old-age payment that reaches citizens regardless of formal employment history. This covers agricultural workers, informal-sector workers, and anyone else outside traditional payroll.
- **A contributory top-up** for formal-sector workers, funded through the same tripartite mechanism (worker + employer contribution, negotiated annually alongside wages) already built for the Nordic-style bargaining system.

### 23.2 Administration

**DG Retrèt ak Sekirite Sosyal** (Retirement & Social Security), added to the Ministry of Universal Health & Social Dignity (Part 5.5) — pairs naturally with the existing single-payer health administration.

## PART 24 — PROCUREMENT RULES

The public ledger (Part 8) publishes contracts once they're signed — but it never specified how a contract gets awarded in the first place, which is where corruption usually actually happens.

### 24.1 Competitive bidding thresholds

Any contract above a defined value must go through open competitive tender, **published on the ledger before award**, not just after — bidders and the public can see who's competing, not only who won.

### 24.2 Debarment

A company or individual found — through the ordinary judiciary, not administrative fiat — to have paid or solicited a bribe is barred from state contracts for a defined term (10 years for a first offense, permanently for repeat offenses), published as a standing public list on the ledger.

### 24.3 Local set-asides

A defined share of total procurement value is reserved for Haitian-owned small businesses and cooperatives (including SEB farmer cooperatives), similar to small-business set-aside programs used in several countries' federal procurement systems — this prevents state spending from defaulting to a handful of large, already-connected firms simply because they're the only ones positioned to win open bids at scale.

## PART 25 — THE ACCOUNTABILITY AUDIT: WHO CHECKS EACH OFFICE, AND THE FIXES THAT CLOSE THE REMAINING GAPS

This section applies one question to every office and mechanism already built: *if this were captured or abused, what stops it — and is that stopping mechanism a different body, selected a different way, on a different timeline, than the thing it's checking?* Where the design already passes, it's listed for completeness. Where it didn't, here is the fix.

### 25.1 What already passes

| Mechanism | The check | Different body? | Different timeline? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Citizens' Assembly | Judiciary can strike its laws; entrenched clauses (Part 19.1) it can't touch | Yes | Yes (12-yr staggered judiciary vs. 2-yr Assembly) |
| Judiciary | Vertical appeals; Ethics Commission finding + 2/3 Assembly for removal | Yes | Yes |
| DSI (freeze/seize/detain power) | Referral-only — judiciary must issue the warrant | Yes | Yes |
| Border Tactical Shield | Judicial emergency review + automatic sunset | Yes | Yes |
| Judicial Nominating Council | Assembly must confirm by two-thirds | Yes | Yes |
| Press & Digital Rights Commission | Cross-selected board + appeal to Tribinal Administratif | Yes | Yes |

### 25.2 The fixes

**1. Mid-term removal of a corrupt Assembly member.** Previously undefined — only future lottery eligibility was addressed, not a currently-serving member. **Fix**: DSI referral → Tribinal Administratif finding → removal decided by a fresh, lottery-drawn Ethics Panel of Assembly members *not* under review (never the full body voting on its own colleague). The vacated seat is refilled by a new sortition draw from the same commune, not the next name on a list — preserving the lottery's integrity rather than creating a backdoor appointment. The removed member is separately referred to ordinary criminal proceedings if warranted.

**2. Mid-term removal of an Executive Council member.** Same gap, same fix pattern: DSI referral → judiciary finding → Assembly removal vote (simple majority, since judicial cause has already been established) → replacement elected by the Assembly per the original selection process.

**3. The Judicial Ethics Commission was judiciary-selected — judges checking judges.** **Fix**: reconstitute it on the same cross-selection logic as the Judicial Nominating Council, but as a genuinely separate body: peer jurists + sortition citizens + law faculty representatives, so judicial misconduct isn't investigated solely by other judges.

**4. Nobody watched the DSI's own conduct.** An anti-corruption agency with unchecked discretion over who it targets is the exact failure mode this document exists to prevent. **Fix**: a cross-selected **DSI Oversight Commission** (sortition citizens + a judicial representative + an Assembly-appointed member) reviews DSI targeting patterns on a regular cadence, takes complaints from anyone alleging improper targeting, and refers findings to the judiciary. DSI investigators themselves are subject to the same lifestyle audits they run on others.

**5. The NASA exam's blind grading had no audit of the blinding itself.** Anonymization only works if it can't be quietly defeated. **Fix**: an independent panel periodically re-grades a random sample blind and compares results, flagging statistical anomalies (a grader consistently deviating, unusual score clustering) — published on the ledger.

**6. The Cyber-Defense Directorate's retaliatory authority had no sign-off.** A significant, escalatory power was sitting inside one ministry's technical unit with no external check before use. **Fix**: Executive Council authorization required before any offensive cyber action, plus mandatory judicial review within 30 days — the same emergency-then-ratify pattern used for disaster and Tactical Shield powers elsewhere in this document.

**7. Central bank / monetary policy had no independence structure at all.** Currency and interest-rate decisions sat inside the Ministry of Economy, directly answerable to whichever Assembly happened to be sitting — a well-documented failure mode on its own; legislative pressure for short-term monetary expansion is a significant contributor to the worst modern inflation crises (Zimbabwe, Argentina, Turkey). **Fix**: an independent **Central Bank Governor**, fixed staggered term (8 years, deliberately not aligned to the 2-year Assembly cycle), selected through the same cross-selected nomination model used for judges — senior economists elected by peers, sortition citizens, and Assembly confirmation by supermajority. Removable only through the same Ethics Commission-plus-supermajority pattern as a judge, never by ordinary political disagreement over interest rates.

**8. The Diaspora Tribunes have an exposure nothing in the design addressed.** They live under a foreign government's jurisdiction, which gives that government direct leverage — visa status, residency — that it has no equivalent hold over an official living in Haiti. **Partial fix, stated honestly as such**: pursue diplomatic-note agreements with major host countries for limited protections while conducting official business, publish all Tribune communications and records on the same open ledger as everyone else (reducing the value of quiet coercion, since there's nothing hidden to pressure them into hiding), and give any Tribune facing credible evidence of host-country pressure the right to request reassignment or rotation without penalty. This reduces the exposure; it does not eliminate it, because it depends partly on a foreign government's cooperation that Haiti cannot compel.

### 25.3 The two that stay as accepted limits, not oversights

**9. The Constituent Assembly (Part 18) has no prior check by definition** — there is no earlier constitution to test a founding moment against. This is true of every constitutional founding in history, not a flaw unique to this design. Broad-based composition, external observation, and supermajority self-certification reduce the risk; nothing removes it entirely.

**10. A Communal Assembly under active gang control can be intimidated in ways no organizational rule reaches.** Per Part 18.4, this document's institutions depend on the underlying security situation improving first in those areas — that is a sequencing dependency to state plainly, not a gap a better rule could close from within the design itself.

## PART 26 — POLICE FORCE RECONSTRUCTION

### 26.1 The honest baseline

Haiti's National Police currently has roughly 13,500 officers for a population of about 11.9 million, and as of mid-2025, fewer than 300 of Haiti's 570 communal sections had any police presence at all. The UN's own assessment puts the actual need closer to 19,000+ officers for comparable coverage. Gangs currently control up to 90% of Port-au-Prince and are expanding into the Centre and Artibonite departments. A UN-authorized Gang Suppression Force (authorized at 5,500 personnel; fewer than 1,000 deployed as of July 2026, with the first Chadian troops arriving April 1, 2026) operates alongside the PNH under an explicitly transitional, renewable mandate, most recently extended to March 31, 2027. Any design that doesn't start from these numbers is designing for a country that doesn't currently exist.

### 26.2 Recruitment and vetting pipeline

- **Target**: grow the PNH toward 19,000–20,000 sworn officers over a defined multi-year window, building directly on the existing P4000 program (4,000 recruits over 18 months, first cohort of 877 already graduated) rather than replacing it.
- **Vetting**: every new recruit passes through this document's NASA-style blind psychometric and integrity screening (Part 17) before commissioning — the goal is a larger force that is also more resistant to gang infiltration and extortion, not just a bigger force.
- **Anti-isolation rule** (Part 17-adjacent principle applied here): officers rotate periodically through community-facing postings rather than remaining permanently in a single specialized unit, reducing the conditions under which an officer becomes personally entangled with a local criminal network.

### 26.3 Phased territorial rollout

Deployment follows wherever GSF/PNH operations actually clear and hold territory, commune by commune — consistent with Part 18.4's "sequencing follows security" principle. A commitment to police presence "everywhere, immediately" would be dishonest given the current 300-of-570 baseline; the honest commitment is a published, ledger-tracked rollout map showing exactly which communes have standing police presence and which don't, updated as coverage genuinely expands, rather than a claim of universal coverage that isn't real.

### 26.4 GSF integration and sunset

The GSF is explicitly a support and transitional force, not a permanent one, under UN Security Council authorization with renewable mandates. This document's institutional goal is for a rebuilt, vetted PNH to progressively assume primary responsibility as its own coverage and public trust grow, coordinated through the existing UN Support Office in Haiti (UNSOH) logistics relationship — not an open-ended dependency on foreign forces, but not a premature withdrawal before PNH capacity is actually there either.

### 26.5 Internal structure

- **Judicial Police**: investigations, feeding directly into the independent prosecution service (Part 5.6) — this division already exists in the current PNH and is retained.
- **Administrative Police**: community patrol, the public-facing branch, operating under the off-duty disarmament and biometric weapon-lockup rules established earlier in this document.
- **Inspector-General function**: retained from the existing PNH structure as the internal-affairs check, with findings now feeding directly into the cross-selected DSI Oversight Commission (Part 25.2) rather than staying purely internal — closing the "who checks the police's own internal watchdog" gap.

### 26.6 Added by the October 2026 revision (see Part 44)

Size by a published coverage formula; recruit locally with at least 30% women; tie every salary to a biometric attendance record and audit the force count each year (13,196 on the books versus about 4,000 on duty at the end of 2023 is where ghost positions hide); no foreign command, pay, or personal payments to officers; and a Haitian-paid-salary rule (Part 44.4–44.5).

## PART 27 — DIPLOMATIC CORPS & EMBASSY NETWORK

### 27.1 The honest baseline

Haiti currently maintains roughly 19–27 embassies abroad (counts vary by source) plus a number of consulates, heavily concentrated in the Americas (16–17 posts), with only three missions in Africa (Benin, Morocco, South Africa) and five in Asia (Japan, Qatar, Taiwan, Vietnam, plus a China commercial office). Worth stating plainly and without taking a side on it: Haiti's diplomatic recognition in Asia currently runs through Taiwan rather than mainland China — a real, consequential choice with real trade-offs either way.

### 27.2 A tiered network, not universal coverage

An embassy in every country is a symbolic footprint Haiti's budget can't justify. A tiered structure matches diplomatic presence to actual stakes:

| Tier | Posts | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — Full embassy, maximum staffing** | United States (+ existing 6 consulates: Atlanta, Boston, Chicago, Miami, NYC, Orlando), Canada, Dominican Republic (+ existing 4 border consulates: Barahona, Dajabón, Higüey, Santiago), France, Brazil, Chile, Bahamas | Largest diaspora populations, or the single most operationally critical relationship (DR) |
| **2 — New posts, making existing strategy real** | India, South Korea, and an African Union-adjacent mission (Addis Ababa) | Part 10.D's "Asymmetric Alliances" strategy names these partners explicitly but Haiti currently has no presence in any of them — without these posts, that strategy is a paragraph, not a policy |
| **3 — Consulate or honorary-consul only** | Remaining Caribbean/Latin American states with smaller Haitian communities | Cost-efficient presence where full embassy staffing isn't justified by volume |

### 27.3 Staffing through the existing pipeline

All diplomatic staff are NASA Foreign Service Track graduates (Part 5.8, Part 17.2) — funded professional training with a service bond, the same model used elsewhere in the civil service, not political appointment. New posts open only when the underlying trade, diaspora, or security relationship justifies the fixed cost, reviewed against the annual budget cycle (Part 12), not opened as a one-time symbolic gesture.

### 27.4 Consular services for the diaspora specifically

Every Tier 1 post functions as more than a formal diplomatic office — it's a physical access point for: passport and civil-documentation services, a direct GLDD legal-assistance intake point (Part on the Dominican Republic legal defense work), and the local liaison for the Brain-Gain Diaspora Fund and the Continental Tribunes (Part 1). Given how central the diaspora is to this entire document's economic strategy, the embassy network is designed as infrastructure for that relationship, not just formal state-to-state representation.

## PART 28 — AGRICULTURAL SELF-SUFFICIENCY TARGETS

### 28.1 Core staple basket, not universal self-sufficiency

Full self-sufficiency across all food categories isn't realistic or economically efficient for any country — comparative advantage still matters, and Haiti will always trade for variety. The honest target is resilience in a defined **core staple basket**: rice, plantain, cassava, yams, maize, beans, and basic poultry/eggs. This is a narrower, more achievable goal than the earlier draft's blanket framing, and it's the one that actually matters for food-security resilience against external shocks.

### 28.2 Phased targets

| Phase | Timeline | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 | Months 1–6 | Departmental crop-specialization zones mapped and operational (Guyana-model, already established); SEB guaranteed-purchase infrastructure begins in the Artibonite |
| Phase 2 | Years 1–3 | Defined reduction in staple import dependency — rice specifically, given the documented collapse of domestic rice production after the 1994–95 tariff reduction is the clearest historical case in this document |
| Phase 3 | Years 3–5 | Majority domestic self-sufficiency across the full core staple basket |

### 28.3 Seed sovereignty — a missing input, added here

A **state seed bank**, distinct from the SPS import-testing standard (that's a public-health regulation; this is a production-strategy input, and the two shouldn't be confused). Purpose: prevent dependency on foreign seed patents and insulate domestic production from external seed-price volatility — a lever several developing agricultural economies have used specifically to protect smallholder farmers from being locked into a single foreign supplier.

### 28.4 The precondition this depends on

Self-sufficiency and land reform are not separate projects. A farmer won't invest in irrigation, soil quality, or multi-year crops on land they don't have a secure claim to — Part 20's national cadastre isn't a nice-to-have alongside this agricultural strategy, it's a load-bearing precondition for it.

## PART 29 — DOMINICAN REPUBLIC RELATIONS: BORDER, COMMERCE, AND RESPONSE

This is a live situation, not a hypothetical: well over 600,000 people have been deported from the DR to Haiti since late 2024 by the Dominican authorities' own counts (about 380,000 in 2025 and more than 226,500 from January to July 2026), including documented cases of pregnant women, nursing mothers, and unaccompanied minors deported despite the DR's own law nominally protecting those groups. The design below has to hold two things at once: real border and commercial sovereignty, and a response mechanism disciplined enough that it can't be turned into a personal or corrupt tool later.

### 29.1 Full border control — the operational detail

- **The four checkpoints** (Ouanaminthe, Belladère, Malpasse, Anse-à-Pitres) house the full Border Tactical Shield presence: digital cargo scanners, the dedicated arms-interdiction unit (Part 10.C, distinct from food/SPS inspection), and biometric processing capacity for both goods and people.
- **Command structure**: DG Sekirite Fwontyè (Part 5.6), under the Ministry of Justice & Public Security, with the same sunset-and-judicial-review constraint on any internal deployment established in Part 2.8 — full operational control, not unchecked control. **Revised by Part 44.3:** this civilian command controls **the Gates** (the four crossings). **The Line** between the crossings is the army's Border Command (1987 Constitution, Art. 266), which stops anyone caught crossing outside a Gate and hands them to the nearest civilian authority within 24 hours.
- **Customs authority**: the AG Douan directorate (Part 5.1) has full authority to inspect, tax, and reject non-compliant goods at these points — this is ordinary, complete sovereign border control, and nothing about it requires special justification, unlike the trade-suspension question below.

### 29.2 Commerce regulation — the standing rules

- The 50% tariff on Dominican goods (established earlier in this document) remains the standing baseline, **with a standing legal-risk flag**: a tariff aimed at one country conflicts with the World Trade Organization's most-favored-nation rule unless it rests on an exception, a safeguard, or the CARICOM–DR framework. Part 45.3 sets out the origin-neutral safety, organic and conformity standards and the reciprocity route, and requires a published legal opinion before any discriminatory measure is used.
- **Labor Reciprocity Enforcement**: any Dominican company employing Haitian workers must hold a state-audited contract guaranteeing international labor standards, fair wages, and medical insurance — enforced by the GLDD, published on the ledger, reviewable annually.

### 29.3 The graduated response mechanism — triggered by documented human rights violations, discrimination, and abuse

Here is the actual fix for responding to genuine abuse without handing any future official unchecked personal discretion: speed matched to severity, and a trigger defined by documented human rights violations, discriminatory enforcement, and abuse — not sentiment.

**What counts as a trigger, defined precisely:**

- **Human rights violations**: unlawful detention, custodial violence, deaths in custody, deportation of legally protected groups (pregnant women, nursing mothers, unaccompanied minors, the elderly) in violation of the DR's own stated law.
- **Discrimination**: enforcement or raids targeting people by skin color, appearance, or perceived ethnicity rather than documented immigration status — this is a real, historically documented pattern in the DR-Haiti relationship, not a hypothetical category.
- **Abuse**: extortion by border or immigration officials, raids on protected sites (hospitals, maternity wards, schools), and any pattern showing state policy or tolerance rather than an isolated incident by a rogue official.

| Response tier | Trigger | Who can act | Speed | Check |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Tier 1 — Formal protest + tariff escalation up to 100%** | A documented individual incident in any of the three categories above (via GLDD case records) | Executive Council | Immediate | Logged publicly at once; Assembly reviews within 2 weeks (same pattern as disaster powers) |
| **Tier 2 — Suspension of a specific trade category or checkpoint** | A documented pattern (multiple incidents, or breach of the Labor Reciprocity agreements) in any of the three categories | Executive Council, with a formal GLDD evidence file attached | Within 48 hours of the pattern being documented | Assembly ratification required within 2 weeks to continue past that window |
| **Tier 3 — Full commercial suspension** | **Systemic** abuse: a documented pattern in any category, **corroborated by at least one independent source** (UN OHCHR, the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights — both bodies with direct standing given Haiti and the DR are both OAS members — or a recognized international human rights organization), not Haiti's assertion alone | Requires the same two-thirds Assembly vote used for any sovereignty-affecting decision (Part 11) | A special emergency Assembly session may be convened within 72 hours given the gravity of a Tier 3 trigger — the check stays intact, but doesn't have to wait for the ordinary legislative calendar | Two-thirds vote required regardless of urgency |

The dual-sourcing requirement at Tier 3 isn't a limit on Haiti's sovereignty — it's what makes the response *harder for the DR to dismiss as a political pretext* and harder for any future Haitian official to misuse for an unrelated purpose. A trade suspension backed by GLDD's own documentation plus an independent international human rights body's corroboration carries far more diplomatic weight, and is far more defensible under international scrutiny, than one backed by Haiti's word alone — which matters directly for the "not backing down, but not isolated either" posture built into Part 11.

### 29.4 Receiving deportees

A dedicated Reception & Reintegration function at all four checkpoints, working alongside the civil-society organizations already doing this work (rather than displacing them) — registration, medical triage tied into the 30-minute clinic network (Part 5.5), and fast-track civil documentation, since many deportees are Haitian citizens who lack formal identification. Staffed by Ajan Seksyon Kominal (Part 4, Tier 7) at the border communal sections.

### 29.5 Haiti's own entry policy — deliberately not a mirror

Haiti's entry criteria for foreign nationals, including Dominicans, are uniform and non-discriminatory by design — explicitly **not** a retaliatory mirror of the documented abuses on the other side. No raids on protected groups, no summary deportation, no detention of pregnant women or unaccompanied minors. Any domestic immigration enforcement inside Haiti goes through the ordinary judiciary, never summary action. What legitimately tracks the DR's conduct is the public, ledger-logged reciprocity clause on visa and entry terms described in 29.3 — calibrated and evidenced, not punitive by nationality. This isn't a moral nicety layered on top of the design; it's the same due-process principle running through Part 14 and everywhere else in this document, applied consistently rather than suspended when it's inconvenient.

## PART 30 — SOUTH-SOUTH AND DIASPORA SOLIDARITY: DIVERSIFICATION, NOT OPPOSITION

This pillar exists for the same structural reason as the correspondent-banking redundancy in Part 15 and the trade diversification in Part 10.A: relying on any single relationship, with any single power, is a vulnerability regardless of that power's intentions. It is explicitly designed to be additive, not adversarial — and the design choices below aren't diplomatic softening layered on top of a harder underlying strategy. They're the actual substance of why this approach is more durable, not less.

### 30.1 The governing principle, modeled on a real historical template

The 1955 Bandung Conference founded what became the Non-Aligned Movement specifically so newly independent nations could cooperate with each other without being read as an anti-Western bloc — its core principles were mutual respect for sovereignty and territorial integrity, non-aggression, non-interference in internal affairs, and equality between states regardless of size. This document adopts the same framing, applied evenhandedly: **Part 30 relationships operate on identical principles to the Tier 1 US relationship in Part 27 — courteous, transparent, sovereignty-respecting — not as an alternative to it.**

### 30.2 What this pillar explicitly is

- **Economic and cultural diversification**: deepened trade and investment ties with CARICOM (already Tier 1, Part 27), the African Union (already a new Tier 2 post, Part 27), India, South Korea, and other post-colonial and majority-Black nations — extending the same redundancy logic already applied to correspondent banking and trade partners to diplomatic relationships generally.
- **A Pan-Diaspora Cultural & Economic Forum**: scholarship exchanges, cultural diplomacy, and trade facilitation with other nations of the African diaspora and post-colonial states — soft-power and economic cooperation, administered through the existing Sovereign Diplomatic Corps (Part 5.8).
- **Multilateral, not ad hoc**: pursued through existing, recognized international bodies — the UN, the OAS, CARICOM, the AU — rather than new, exclusive pacts. Participation in institutions the whole world already recognizes reads as ordinary diplomacy; a new standalone alliance structure reads as something else entirely, regardless of intent.

### 30.3 What this pillar explicitly is not

**No military or security dimension of any kind.** This is a hard line, not a nuance — an economic and cultural solidarity network with a security component is a fundamentally different, and far more provocative, thing than one without it. Haiti's existing security relationships (the GSF, Part 26) stay exactly where they are, unaffected by and unconnected to this pillar.

### 30.4 Radical transparency as the actual protection

Every agreement, meeting record, and funding flow connected to this pillar is published on the same open public ledger (Part 8) as everything else in this document — in real time, not after the fact. This matters for a specific reason: historically, *secrecy* around a small state's foreign relationships has been what gets read as threatening, far more than the relationships themselves. A publicly auditable partnership can't be characterized as a hidden pact, because there's nothing hidden to characterize that way.

### 30.5 The US relationship stays coequal, not subordinate

To be explicit rather than implicit about this: the United States remains a Tier 1 relationship under Part 27, with the largest diaspora population, the largest trading relationship, and the current primary security partnership (Part 26). Part 30 does not deprioritize that relationship — it reduces the risk of any single relationship, US or otherwise, becoming a point of total dependency, which is a resilience principle, not a realignment.

### 30.6 Why this posture is the safer one, not a compromise

The strongest protection against external destabilization isn't a cleverer alliance structure — it's the domestic legitimacy architecture already built across this entire document: an independent judiciary, a transparent public ledger, no single office capable of unilateral action, and now a foreign policy that is itself fully public. A government that is genuinely transparent, broadly legitimate, and visibly non-aligned against any specific power is a fundamentally harder target to justify hostile action against than one that can be plausibly characterized as secretly realigning — which is precisely why every mechanism in this pillar is designed to be public by default.

## PART 31 — UNIVERSAL HEALTH INSURANCE & HOSPITAL MODERNIZATION (MODELED ON JAPAN'S SYSTEM)

This section deliberately copies the specific mechanisms that make Japan's system work — not just the general idea of "universal healthcare," which many designs claim, but the particular structural features most of them skip.

### 31.1 Universal mandatory enrollment — no opt-out

Every citizen and legal resident is automatically enrolled based on employment status or residency, the same way Japan's system works — enrollment isn't a choice made at a moment in time, it's a default nobody has to act to receive.

### 31.2 Two insurance tracks, mirroring Japan's actual dual structure

- **Employees' Health Insurance track** (mirrors Japan's *Shakai Hoken*): for formal-sector workers. Premium is a percentage of income, split between employer and employee, deducted through payroll — set and revised through the same tripartite bargaining body already handling wages (Part 6 of the earlier design) and pensions (Part 23).
- **Commune Health Insurance track** (mirrors Japan's *Kokumin Kenko Hoken*, which Japan administers at the municipal level — a direct structural match for Haiti's existing commune architecture): for the self-employed, agricultural workers, and the informal-sector majority. Premiums are assessed by income tier and household size, collected through the Sovereign House network — and the Pix-inspired domestic payment system discussed in the prior turn is a natural collection rail for this, solving two design problems with one piece of infrastructure.
- **A subsidized floor**, funded from general and resource revenue: premiums scale down to zero for those below a defined income threshold, the same non-contributory logic already built for the pension floor (Part 23.1). Nobody is denied enrollment for inability to pay.

### 31.3 The National Fee Schedule — the mechanism most designs skip, and the one that actually controls cost

Japan's core cost-control tool isn't rationing — it's a **single, nationally uniform price list** for every procedure, drug, and service, negotiated every two years between the health ministry, a physician representative body, and civil-service economists, and applied identically at every hospital and clinic in the country. No hospital can charge more than another for the same procedure; no patient can be charged a different price based on region, insurer, or negotiating leverage.

**Direct copy**: a **National Health Fee Schedule Commission** — tripartite-structured like the wage-bargaining body — sets and revises Haiti's fee schedule every two years. Every DG Sèvis Sante Kominotè clinic and every departmental surgical hospital (Part 5.5) is bound to the same published price list, on the open ledger, for the same service.

### 31.4 The out-of-pocket cap — the anti-bankruptcy mechanism

This is the single most important, and most frequently skipped, feature of Japan's system: a **monthly ceiling on out-of-pocket costs**, scaled by income tier. Once a patient's payments in a given month exceed their tier's ceiling, the state covers everything further — regardless of how expensive the underlying treatment is. This is what prevents a serious illness from becoming a bankruptcy event, which flat co-pay percentages alone don't protect against.

**Direct copy**: a monthly out-of-pocket cap, set by income tier (using the same income-tier framework as the Commune Health Insurance track), administered by DG Sistèm Sante Inifye (Part 5.5) and enforced automatically through the same ledger that tracks every other transaction in this document — the cap triggers without the patient needing to apply for relief after the fact.

### 31.5 Standardized, portable benefits — free choice of provider

Japan places no network restrictions on patients: any enrollee can go to any hospital or clinic in the country, with identical coverage, regardless of which insurance track they're on. This prevents the two-tier outcome — rich regions getting better coverage than poor ones — that a commune-by-commune benefit design would otherwise risk.

**Direct copy**: the benefit package is identical nationwide regardless of which track a citizen is enrolled in or which commune they live in. A citizen enrolled through a rural Commune Health Insurance office has access to the exact same departmental surgical hospital, on the exact same terms, as someone on the formal-sector track in Port-au-Prince.

### 31.6 Non-profit hospital ownership — copied directly

Japanese law prohibits for-profit corporations from owning hospitals; they must be owned by non-profit medical corporations or the state. This keeps clinical decisions clinical rather than shareholder-driven, and keeps the fee schedule's price controls from being circumvented by extracting profit elsewhere in the system.

**Direct copy**: hospitals and clinics operating within the single-payer network must be owned by the state or by a registered non-profit medical entity — no for-profit corporate hospital ownership within the universal system. Private, fully out-of-pocket clinics may still exist alongside it, but cannot bill the universal insurance system.

### 31.7 Age-tiered cost-sharing

Japan reduces the co-pay percentage for children and the elderly relative to working-age adults, recognizing that these groups both need care more and can absorb costs less. **Direct copy**: lower co-pay tiers for children and the elderly than for working-age adults, within the same fee-schedule structure.

### 31.8 Hospital modernization and staffing

- **One flagship specialty hospital per department** (10 total, at chef-lieu level per the existing territorial structure), funded through binding multi-year appropriations (Part 13) so construction survives an Assembly turnover, with diaspora bonds as a funding channel.
- **Staffing** through the funded-training-for-service-bond model (Part 17.2): the state pays for the medical degree, the doctor serves a fixed term in return, combined with the Brain-Gain Fund's mandate to recruit diaspora specialists back with competitive salaries.

### 31.9 Why this doesn't carry the Pix-style risk

A domestic health insurance and hospital system is a lower-profile target than a payments rail, because it does not threaten an incumbent's transaction-fee income. It is not risk-free, though. A national fee schedule squeezes drug, device and insurer margins, and a non-profit-only hospital rule affects foreign hospital operators, so expect pushback on medicine prices. Publish how drugs are procured and negotiated, and do not describe this program as free of external risk.

## PART 32 — THE NATIONAL PAYMENT SYSTEM

References to a Pix-inspired domestic payment rail have appeared in Parts 15, 23, and 31 without ever being built out as its own system. Here it is, consolidated — with one architectural correction that matters for actually copying Pix's design rather than just its ambition.

### 32.1 Scope: a domestic settlement rail, not a cross-border one

This is worth stating as a hard boundary, not a footnote, given the caution established in Part 15 and the live Pix/US tariff case discussed earlier: **this system settles payments inside Haiti, between Haitian accounts, in Sovereign Gourde. It is not built, marketed, or positioned as a cross-border alternative to correspondent banking or SWIFT** — that risk category stays exactly where Part 15 put it: watched, piloted cautiously, never a named policy pillar. Conflating the two would undo the exact caution that came out of the Pix/Brazil discussion.

### 32.2 The architectural correction: a rail, not an app

Pix's actual design isn't a single government-run consumer app — it's an **interbank instant-settlement rail** operated by Brazil's central bank, which any bank or licensed fintech connects to, each running its own customer-facing app on top of the same underlying pipe. That distinction matters: a single state-run monopoly app would crowd out Haiti's existing financial institutions and would be worse design, not more faithful to the model it's copying.

**Direct copy**: the independent Central Bank (Part 25.2, fix #7) operates the underlying settlement rail — **Rezo Peman Nasyonal (RPN)**. Existing Haitian financial institutions connect to it as participants, keeping their own customer-facing products: commercial banks, the *kès popilè* credit unions that already serve a large share of Haitians without traditional bank access, and existing mobile money providers. The state builds and secures the pipe; it doesn't have to displace what's already running on top of similar pipes.

### 32.3 Access — the same three-tier model already built for the ledger

Reusing Part 8.2's access design rather than inventing a separate one:

1. **App-based**, for anyone with a smartphone and data, through any participating institution's app.
2. **USSD/SMS**, for basic phones with no data plan.
3. **A physical terminal with an agent at every Sovereign House**, for citizens without a phone at all — the same commune-level infrastructure already serving the ledger and the 30-minute clinic network.

Transfers use a simple key — phone number or national ID — rather than requiring full bank account details, the same simplicity that made Pix's adoption rate what it is.

### 32.4 What runs through it

Consolidating what's already been referenced elsewhere in this document into one list:

- Civil service salary disbursement, at every grade (Part 7)
- Tax and premium collection, feeding directly into the open ledger (Part 8) and the annual budget cycle (Part 12)
- SEB guaranteed farmer payments (Part 5.2) — instant payment at the point of sale, replacing the security risk of cash payment in rural areas
- Pension contributions and disbursement, both tracks (Part 23)
- Health insurance premiums and provider reimbursement, both tracks (Part 31)
- The final-mile leg of diaspora remittances — once a transfer crosses the border through existing correspondent channels (Part 15) and converts to Sovereign Gourde, its last leg to a rural recipient's phone runs on this rail instead of requiring a physical remittance-agent visit

### 32.5 Framing and pace

Given the Pix/Brazil case discussed directly before this: the RPN is framed and scaled from the outset as **financial inclusion infrastructure** — banking the unbanked, replacing risky cash handling for farmers and remittance recipients — not as a challenge to any existing commercial network's revenue. At Haiti's actual economic scale this is unlikely to draw the kind of attention Brazil's system did regardless, but the framing is deliberate rather than accidental, consistent with the caution already built into Part 15.

## PART 33 — MILITARY, SECURITY-SECTOR CONSOLIDATION, AND POLICE REEDUCATION

### 33.1 The fragmentation problem, named plainly

Haiti currently has several distinct armed institutions with separate chains of command: the PNH (roughly 10,000–13,500 officers, with specialized sub-units like riot control and intervention brigades), the reconstituted Armed Forces (FAd'H, roughly 1,000–1,500 personnel across army, a nascent navy, and an aviation corps), the international Gang Suppression Force, and a large, largely unregulated private security industry that wealthy individuals and businesses rely on given weak state coverage. This fragmentation is itself part of the accountability problem — unclear command lines make it easy for responsibility to disappear between institutions. Every armed institution in this design reports through one of two clear chains: the Ministry of Justice & Public Security (police, border, private security licensing) or the Ministry of Foreign Affairs & Sovereign Defense (military) — both already built in Part 5 — with no third, unaccounted-for channel.

### 33.2 The military — history-informed constitutional constraints

This is not a hypothetical risk for Haiti; it's the country's own direct history. The FAd'H was **completely disbanded in 1995 specifically because of its record of coups and abuses**, after decades in which the military — which historically included the police as a single entangled institution until reforms separated them — played an outsized and often destructive role in Haitian political life. It was reconstituted in 2017 and currently stands at roughly 1,000–1,500 personnel, with a stated government goal of expanding toward 6,000 in the near term and 20,000 over four years. Given that history, expansion has to come with constitutional limits at least as strong as the growth itself:

- **No independent domestic policing authority.** The military's role is constitutionally limited to border defense (the Line, Part 44.3), disaster response (Part 5.6's civil protection function), and support to the PNH — and support means exactly that: it cannot conduct independent domestic security operations without civilian authorization from the Ministry of Justice & Public Security for each deployment, logged on the ledger.
- **The Commander-in-Chief appointment is not a single presidential-style decision.** It follows the same cross-selected nomination logic already used for judges and the Central Bank Governor (Part 25.2, fix #7) — a Defense Nominating Council (senior officers elected by peers + Assembly-appointed civilians) proposes, the Assembly confirms by supermajority, and removal follows the same Ethics Commission-plus-supermajority pattern used elsewhere. No single office can build personal loyalty within the military's top command.
- **Budget and personnel growth run through the same annual budget cycle and DSI oversight as any other institution** (Parts 12 and 25.2) — expansion is deliberate and tracked, not ad hoc.
- **This is the clause that matters most, and it's entrenched (Part 19.1):** the prohibition on independent domestic military policing authority is added to the list of entrenched constitutional clauses that cannot be amended by ordinary supermajority — only by the highest bar in the document. Haiti has already lived through what happens when this isn't locked down; it shouldn't be one ordinary vote away from repeating it.

### 33.3 Police reeducation — the actual curriculum, not just the intent

Part 26 already built the recruitment, vetting, and phased-deployment pipeline. This is the missing piece: what retraining actually contains, for officers already serving.

**The evidence-based model**: procedural justice training — a well-documented policing curriculum built around four elements shown in research to reduce use-of-force incidents and rebuild community trust: giving citizens genuine voice during any interaction, neutral and consistent application of rules, treating people with dignity regardless of the encounter's outcome, and demonstrating trustworthy motives rather than pure enforcement posture. This isn't a values statement — it's a specific, trainable curriculum with a real track record.

**A real comparative case, adapted rather than copied wholesale**: Georgia's 2004 police reform is the most-cited example of turning around an endemically corrupt and abusive force — the government fired the entire ~15,000-member traffic police in a single day and rebuilt a smaller, dramatically better-paid, rigorously vetted force from scratch, cutting bribery sharply within a short period. The *insight* worth taking — higher pay plus real vetting plus a smaller, better-trained force beats a larger, underpaid, unaccountable one — is sound. The *method* isn't directly transferable: Georgia wasn't simultaneously facing armed groups controlling most of its capital. A mass firing in Haiti's current security crisis would create a governance vacuum more dangerous than the corruption it solved, which is exactly why Part 26 already committed to phased, individual vetting rather than a purge. Georgia's lesson informs the pay-and-training design; Haiti's own current conditions determine the pace.

**Concretely, added to the Part 26 pipeline**: every serving officer — not just new recruits — passes through mandatory procedural-justice retraining within a defined window, with continued service conditional on completing it; bodycam footage (already required, earlier in this document) is sampled and reviewed against procedural-justice standards, not just used reactively after a complaint; and officers who complete retraining and maintain a clean review record become eligible for the same pay-grade improvements Georgia's reform used as the other half of the equation — better conduct is met with better pay, not just better rules.

### 33.4 Private security regulation

Currently a large, under-regulated sector in Haiti. **A licensing and oversight regime**, administered by the Ministry of Justice & Public Security: mandatory registration, the same NASA-adjacent background vetting standard used for public officers, a firearms registry cross-checked against the biometric lockup system's logic (Part 1), and private security firms barred from operating in any capacity that resembles independent domestic policing — the same boundary drawn around the military in 33.2 applies here for the same reason.

### 33.5 Border protection — consolidated reference

Already built in operational detail in Part 29.1 — the Border Tactical Shield under DG Sekirite Fwontyè. **Revised by Part 44.3:** the Border Tactical Shield and customs hold authority at **the Gates** (inspection, taxation, migration, humanitarian reception); the army's Border Command holds **the Line** between the Gates, which is external defense and frontier surveillance under 1987 Art. 266, not domestic policing. The two chains are separate and meet only at a logged 24-hour handover, which keeps the single-clear-chain principle and keeps the army out of customs and trade.

### 33.6 Tourism's real dependency

Part 5.7 already built the tourism ministry structure — 51% Haitian ownership, community revenue-sharing — but tourism development doesn't happen independently of the security reforms in this section. High-value eco-tourism in Jacmel or the northern historic circuit requires the phased territorial security rollout described in Part 26.3 to actually reach those regions first. This isn't a separate policy track; it's downstream of everything else in this Part.

## PART 34 — HOUSING: THE VIENNA MECHANISM, ADAPTED TO HAITI'S ACTUAL CONDITIONS

### 34.1 The real starting point

Haiti's housing crisis has several distinct, overlapping layers: informal settlements dating from before and after the 2010 earthquake, many without secure tenure or basic services; over a million people internally displaced by gang violence in 2025 alone; deportees arriving from the DR (Part 29.4) with nowhere to land; and a construction sector still shaped by the building-code failures that made the 2010 earthquake so much deadlier than the seismic event itself required. A housing policy has to address all four, not just "build more units."

### 34.2 The Vienna mechanism, not the Vienna price tag

Vienna's actual insight isn't "spend a lot on housing" — it's that **a large, permanent, non-speculative housing stock, with rents locked to cost rather than market price and surplus reinvested rather than extracted, caps what the entire market can charge**, not just what the subsidized units cost. That mechanism is transferable at any income level; a €600,000 Viennese apartment is not.

**Sovereign Housing Cooperatives (Kooperativ Lojman Souvren)** — Haiti's adapted version: cost-based contribution scaled to income (reusing the same income-tier framework already built for health insurance in Part 31 and pensions in Part 23, rather than inventing a new one), permanent asset lock (units can never be privately resold, generation after generation, exactly copying Vienna's core rule), and any surplus reinvested into new construction or maintenance — but built to Haiti's actual construction economics and materials, not imported European standards.

### 34.3 The precondition this depends on

This cannot outrun Part 20. Large-scale formal housing construction on land with unclear or disputed title just recreates the land-tenure crisis at a larger scale — the cadastre and community-based adjudication process has to run ahead of, or at minimum alongside, any major housing push, commune by commune.

### 34.4 Upgrade what exists before building what doesn't

The largest share of Haiti's housing problem isn't a gap to fill with new construction — it's existing informal settlements that need service and formalization, not demolition. The internationally recommended approach for this (used successfully in various Latin American and Caribbean informal-settlement contexts) is **in-situ upgrading**: bringing water, sanitation, and power to where people already live — using the DINEPA-equivalent water directorate and solar micro-grid network already built in Part 5.3 — and formalizing tenure through the cadastre (Part 20), rather than displacing established communities to build something new elsewhere. This is cheaper, faster, and preserves the social networks people already depend on.

### 34.5 Construction standards — the lesson the 2010 earthquake taught at enormous cost

Much of the earthquake's death toll came from building failures that better, still-affordable construction methods would have prevented. **A mandatory seismic- and hurricane-resilient building code**, enforced by NASA-trained engineers (Part 17.2) through the Infrastructure Ministry, using cost-effective methods proven in comparable seismic contexts — confined masonry construction, used successfully in post-earthquake reconstruction in Peru and Colombia, costs little more than standard unreinforced construction but performs dramatically better. This isn't a luxury standard; it's the one place in this housing policy where cutting cost is not an acceptable tradeoff.

### 34.6 A separate track for the current emergency

Displacement from gang violence and DR deportations is happening now, not on the multi-year timeline formal cooperative housing construction requires. A **distinct transitional-housing track**, explicitly designed to graduate people into permanent Sovereign Housing Cooperative units as land tenure and construction capacity catch up — built specifically to avoid the failure mode that followed the 2010 earthquake, where "temporary" camps became semi-permanent, under-serviced settlements for years. Transitional sites are planned from the start with a defined transition timeline and a specific cooperative development pipeline they feed into, not left open-ended.

### 34.7 Financing — channeling an existing pattern, not replacing it

Diaspora-funded home construction is already a large, existing informal channel in Haiti — family members abroad already fund individual home building directly. Rather than competing with this, **diaspora bonds (Part 1's Brain-Gain Fund) get a housing-specific tranche**, giving diaspora investors a way to fund the cooperative system at scale, with the same transparency and permanence guarantees as an individual family home, but contributing to the shared, price-capping stock rather than only individual private construction.

### 34.8 Administration

A new **DG Lojman Sosyal** (Social Housing), added to the Ministry of Infrastructure & Energy Transfer (Part 5.3) — housing sits naturally alongside the physical infrastructure this ministry already builds. It works directly with the Land Cadastre directorate (Part 20, Ministry of Economy) and the Commune Officer network (Part 4) for local implementation, and priority access to the first cooperative units goes to displaced persons and returning deportees before general income-qualified applicants — the emergency comes first, structurally, not just rhetorically.

### 34.9 An honest scale expectation

Vienna took a century to reach 40% non-speculative housing stock, starting from a much higher base income than Haiti has today. This isn't a program that reaches Vienna's scale quickly, and claiming otherwise would repeat the overreach already corrected elsewhere in this document. The realistic target, phased against the existing timeline (Parts 1 and 28): Phase 2 (Years 1–3) focuses on in-situ upgrading and transitional housing for the current displacement crisis; Phase 3 (Years 3–5) begins the first Sovereign Housing Cooperative construction, once the cadastre has cleared title in the pilot communes.

## PART 35 — CHILD PROTECTION: STREET CHILDREN, RESIDENTIAL CARE OVERSIGHT, AND ANTI-EXPLOITATION

This builds directly on the family-protection reforms in Part 14 and the child-welfare directorate in Part 5.5, extending both into a complete protective framework.

### 35.1 Street children and homelessness — family first, institutions last

Haiti has a well-documented, specific form of child exploitation distinct from formal orphanage abuse: the *restavek* system, in which children — overwhelmingly from poor rural families — are sent to live with wealthier households as domestic labor, sometimes never attending school and sometimes abused. This needs its own detection pathway, not just general child-welfare policy.

Following the international child-welfare consensus (UNICEF's care-reform guidance) that institutional placement should be the last resort, not the default:

1. **Family tracing and reunification first** — most street children and *restavek* children have identifiable family; the state's first obligation is locating and safely reuniting, not housing.
2. **Kinship and foster care second**, where reunification isn't safe or possible.
3. **Small, community-based group care third.**
4. **Large residential institutions only as an explicit last resort**, court-ordered per Part 5.5's existing reform, never a default placement.
5. **The compulsory education mandate (Part 5.4) doubles as an early-detection system**: school enrollment verification at the commune level catches children — *restavek* or otherwise — being kept out of school, which is one of the clearest visible markers of a child in an exploitative household situation.
6. **Street outreach teams**, working through the existing Ajan Seksyon Kominal network (Part 4, Tier 7) rather than a separate new bureaucracy, plus a confidential reporting line staffed at every Sovereign House.

### 35.2 Residential care institutions — licensing and oversight

**Superseded in part by Part 40.** An outright ban on foreign-funded orphanages would leave about 30,000 children without a transition plan. Part 40 replaces it with licensing, a National Child Register, funding that follows outcomes rather than beds, Haitian-majority governance with joint liability for the foreign sponsor, a Child-Safe Register, a ban on orphanage tourism and short-term care volunteering, and a sequenced closure of the worst institutions. The oversight below still applies to whatever residential care remains necessary under 35.1's last-resort standard:

- **Mandatory licensing** for any residential child-care institution, state academies included — no institution operates unlicensed.
- **Unannounced inspections**, on a regular cadence, with findings published on the open ledger.
- **NASA-style background vetting** (Part 17) for every staff member with child access, no exceptions.
- **Whistleblower protection** for both staff and children who report concerns, with a reporting channel that bypasses the institution's own management entirely.
- **No unvetted visitor access** — a firm ban on the "orphanage tourism" pattern documented in several countries, where untrained foreign visitors gain access to vulnerable children under the guise of volunteering or donating. Legitimate donors and family members have defined, vetted access channels; casual visitor access does not exist.

### 35.3 A specialized, victim-centered anti-trafficking unit

**Inite Espesyalize Kont Trafik Moun** (Specialized Anti-Human-Trafficking Unit), within the prosecution service (Part 5.6):

- **Victim-centered by design**: victims are never prosecuted for status-related offenses connected to their own exploitation.
- **Specialized, trauma-informed training** for investigators and prosecutors, vetted through the same NASA process as the rest of the judiciary.
- **International cooperation** through the GLDD's existing overseas legal network (Part 5.8), since trafficking routinely crosses borders.
- **Organ trafficking specifically**: mandatory sourcing-verification and reporting protocols for any transplant procedure, enforced through hospital licensing (extending Part 31.6's non-profit ownership rule with an audit requirement specifically for transplant sourcing) — a hospital's non-profit license is contingent on compliance.

### 35.4 "Aid porn" and fundraising ethics — closing a real, documented exploitation pathway

Extending the Press & Digital Rights Commission (Part 6) and the existing Anti-Aid Edict:

- **Consent and dignity standards** for any imagery of children used in fundraising or media — this is a real, internationally documented problem, not a hypothetical one, where vulnerable children's images are used for donor appeals without consent or regard for their dignity.
- **Superseded by Part 40.7.** Routing every child-related donation through the treasury would make the treasury the largest honey pot for capture and cut off honest private giving. Instead, child-focused fundraising is regulated by registration, audit, ledger publication and the Children's Dignity Code (no fundraising images of children in care or crisis without consent and dignity review; honest appeals; right to withdraw). The state regulates the *appeal*, never reporting, opinion or criticism. Independent access plus independent fundraising was the combination that enabled exploitation; vetting (Part 40.4) and registered, audited fundraising (Part 40.7) break it without censorship.

### 35.5 Vetting extends to every child-serving role, not just formal child-welfare staff

Teachers (Part 5.4's National Teaching Corps), pediatric health workers (Part 5.5), and state academy staff all pass through the same NASA-style background vetting as police and civil servants, with mandatory reporting obligations attached to the role itself — reporting suspected abuse isn't discretionary for anyone working with children in any capacity.

## PART 36 — UNIVERSAL BIRTH REGISTRATION AND THE NAMING STANDARD

### 36.1 Universal birth registration

- **Skilled attendance at every birth** — hospital, clinic, or a certified *sage-femme* (midwife) — using the 30-minute clinic network already built (Part 5.5) and the funded-training pipeline (Part 17.2) extended specifically to certify community midwives where clinic access is still developing. This is sound maternal-health policy on its own terms, independent of documentation.
- **Certificate issued at the point of delivery**, by the attending hospital, clinic, or midwife, logged directly into the civil registry administered through the NASA-vetted registrar corps.
- **A 1–3 month parental declaration window** at the commune-level Sovereign House, formally linking the birth certificate to citizenship (Part 22) — with a **penalty-free extension for families facing genuine access barriers** (remote communes, disrupted travel), so the deadline itself never becomes the mechanism that creates an undocumented child.
- This closes, at the source, the exact documentation gap already identified in Part 29.4 — Haitian citizens deported from the DR who lack formal ID despite being citizens. Going forward, that gap stops being created in the first place.

### 36.2 The naming standard — narrow, and about the child, not about origin

The registrar reviews a proposed name against exactly one question: **could this name reasonably be expected to cause the child ridicule, harm, or embarrassment** — not whether the name sounds sufficiently Haitian, foreign, or culturally "appropriate." This mirrors the standard used in the small number of countries that regulate naming at all (Iceland's naming committee, for instance, checks grammatical fit and potential harm to the child, not cultural origin) rather than a broader taste or identity test, which has no clean, non-arbitrary line to draw and would sit uneasily next to the family-autonomy principle already established in Part 14.

- **What's checked**: the name must be registerable in the civil and digital ledger systems (standard alphabet), and must not be reasonably expected to cause the child harm or ridicule — an obscenity, a slur, a number, or similar.
- **What's explicitly not checked**: the name's cultural, linguistic, or national origin. Haitian naming has always drawn on French, Kreyòl, African-derived, Catholic, and global influences — that's the existing, longstanding pattern, not a threat to it.
- **The registrar's decision is appealable** to the Civic Tribunal (Part 2) — no single registrar has unchecked final say over a family's naming decision, consistent with every other discretionary power in this document having a check.

## PART 37 — THE DESSALINES CHARTER: A SYMBOLIC FOUNDATION, NOT A LEGAL MODEL

Haiti is the country where enslaved people defeated an empire and founded a state. A modern constitution that forgets this reads like a foreign-drafted text. This Part puts the 1805 Constitution — the first constitution written after independence, sanctioned by Jean-Jacques Dessalines on 20 May 1805 — at the symbolic center of this system, and is exact about what to carry forward and what to leave in 1805.

### 37.1 What the document actually is

- Signed by the generals of the new state (Christophe, Clerveaux, Vernet, Gabart, Pétion and others), who "submit it to the sanction of H.M. the Emperor Jacques Dessalines." Dessalines accepted and sanctioned it at the Imperial Palace on 20 May 1805, "second year of the Independence of Hayti."
- It is a constitution of an **empire in a state of siege**, written two years after independence under constant threat of French reconquest. Some of its harshest clauses (confiscation, exclusion of former colonists from ownership, military districts) only make sense in that context.
- It is distinct from the **1804 Act of Independence**, which the 1987 Preamble cites by name. The national anthem is already "La Dessalinienne" (1987 Constitution, Art. 4-1).
- **Source caution:** the English text used here is the translation published in the *New York Evening Post* on 15 July 1805 (as hosted on Wikisource). Any official use must start from the French original, not this translation.

### 37.2 How it works as a symbol (institutional, not decorative)

1. **Document 0 on the public ledger.** The Charter is the first entry, above every law, so every citizen who opens the ledger — by web, SMS or terminal at the Sovereign House — sees where the system comes from.
2. **The oath.** Assembly members, Executive Council members, judges, officers and civil servants take an oath that names the Charter's principles (sovereignty, abolition of servitude, equality before the law, a trade for every citizen). Taking it before the community, not in a closed room.
3. **A plaque and a reading in every Sovereign House**, and the first lesson of civic education in every school (Part 5.4).
4. **The Preamble of any new constitution** quotes Articles 1–4 of the Charter directly.
5. **Not a cult of personality.** The 1987 Constitution (Art. 7) bans effigies and names of living persons on public property. The Charter honors principles, not a man's person; the design keeps that line. Dessalines was assassinated on 17 October 1806, and historians widely link that to the tension between his rule and the military and landed elite of the time — a reminder, built into this design, that concentrated power in one hand does not even protect the person holding it.

### 37.3 What to carry forward — and where it lives in this design

| 1805 text | Modern adaptation | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Art. 1** — a state "sovereign and independent of any other power in the universe" | Sovereignty as an operating rule: no foreign command of the public force, no foreign money in campaigns, no unilateral concessions by any one official | Parts 9, 11, 44 |
| **Art. 2** — "Slavery is forever abolished" | Entrenched. Extended to modern servitude: *restavek* (child domestic servitude), trafficking, debt bondage, forced labor | Parts 35, 40; entrenched in 19.1 |

| **Art. 11** — "Every citizen must possess a mechanic art" | **National Skills Guarantee:** every young Haitian leaves school or national service with a certified trade. This is the 1805 root of the skills prisons, gang reintegration and technology hub | Parts 41, 42, 43 |
| **Art. 12** (as a principle) — foreigners may not come as "master or proprietor" | Today expressed by residence and enterprise, not by race: the 1987 limits on foreign land (Arts. 55–55-5), plus a beneficial-owner register and a ban on Haitian "straw-man" fronts | Parts 20, 46 |

### 37.4 What stays in 1805 — and why

- **Absolute power in one office** (Arts. 30–35): the ruler appoints and dismisses all ministers, judges and officers, controls the mint and the treasury, alone makes war and peace, and alone pardons. Everything this document was built to prevent.
- **Judge in his own cause** (General Arts. 3 and 9): treason and "dilapidations of the ministers and generals" are tried by a special council "called and presided by the emperor," and death sentences need his confirmation.
- **Dynastic succession** (Arts. 23–27): the Emperor names his own successor.
- **Loss of citizenship, with death and confiscation, for emigrants** (Art. 7), and the death penalty more broadly (General Art. 8; the 1987 Constitution already abolishes it, Art. 20): the diaspora today is a pillar of this design (Diaspora Tribunes, Brain-Gain Fund). Punishing emigration is the opposite of the policy.
- **Citizenship suspended by bankruptcy** (Art. 8): punishes poverty.
- **A racial legal category** (Arts. 12, 14): equal citizenship cannot depend on color. The 1805 text itself made exceptions by *commitment to liberty*, not skin — it naturalized German and Polish soldiers and white women (Arts. 12–13). That is the principle worth keeping: **citizenship follows loyalty to the republic, not ancestry.**
- **Church-state rules** (Arts. 50–52, no predominant religion and no state funding of religion): worth study, but not adopted here; the 1987 Constitution's religious-freedom framework stays.
- **Military districts as the unit of government** (Arts. 15–17, 53): the civilian territorial design in Part 3 replaces it.

### 37.5 One more symbolic act: the oath's last line

The Charter closes by recommending itself "to our successors" and "to the friends of liberty, to philanthropists of all countries." That is the posture this design wants: proud, sovereign, and open to honest friends. It is the 1805 version of "courteous to every country, deferential to none."

## PART 38 — THE 1987 CONSTITUTION: WHAT TO KEEP, THE LEGAL ROUTE, AND THE REFORM PROPOSALS COMPARED

This Part is based on the English text of the 1987 Constitution as amended through 2012 (the Constitutional Law of 9 May 2011 / 19 June 2012). Article numbers below are from that text.

### 38.1 Where things stand (October 2026)

- The 1987 Constitution is in force. The Council of Ministers abandoned the project to replace it on **9 October 2025**, after more than a year of consultations criticized for cost, doubtful legality and distraction from security and elections. The official line: elections will be held "under the 1987 Constitution, despite all the problems we know it contains." One secondary source says a new attempt was relaunched in February 2026; I could not confirm details, so treat that as unverified.
- The Transitional Presidential Council's mandate ended on 7 February 2026. Prime Minister Alix Didier Fils-Aimé is the sole executive under a political pact among parties and civil-society groups. The first-round election is scheduled for 13 December 2026 (a runoff on 21 February 2027, a government on 12 March 2027); dates have slipped repeatedly.

### 38.2 The legal problem this document must face honestly

The amendment articles are strict, and this design's own amendment machinery (Part 19) and bootstrapping path (Part 18) collide with them:

- **Art. 282–282-1:** an amendment must be declared necessary by two-thirds of *each* chamber, and only "in the course of the last Regular Session of the Legislative period."
- **Art. 283–284-1:** the next legislature meets as a National Assembly, needs two-thirds of the members of each chamber *present*, and two-thirds of the votes cast.
- **Art. 284-2:** an amendment takes effect only after the *next elected President* is installed; the sitting President cannot benefit.
- **Art. 284-3:** "General elections to amend the Constitution by referendum are strictly forbidden." (This was the central legal objection of Haiti's leading human-rights network RNDDH to the 2021 referendum project; it also cited Art. 150, which limits the President to the powers the Constitution gives him, and Arts. 282–284.) Those are advocacy positions, not court rulings, but the text is plain.
- **Art. 284-4:** "No amendment to the Constitution may effect the democratic and republican nature of the State."

What follows for this design, on my reading (a Haitian constitutional lawyer must confirm it):

1. **A referendum as the vehicle to amend the 1987 text is prohibited by its own words.** Part 18's Phase 3 and Part 19's referendum steps cannot ride on an *amendment* of the 1987 Constitution.
2. **A strict amendment path is slow.** There is no sitting Parliament now. The legislature elected in the 2026–27 cycle would have to declare the need in the last regular session of its term, and the *next* legislature would decide — realistically around 2031, with effect after the next elected President is installed, around 2032. This is my reading of the timing, not an official one.
3. **Sortition as the lawmaking body may be argued to touch "the democratic and republican nature of the State" (Art. 284-4)** — a lottery-selected assembly can be defended as deliberative democracy, and it can be attacked as non-electoral. Either argument is available, which is itself the risk.
4. **A full replacement** through a constituent assembly and a referendum — which is how the 1987 Constitution itself was adopted — has no text in the 1987 charter to rely on, and the 2021 and 2025 attempts show it needs broad consensus to survive.

### 38.3 The recommended route: three tracks, in order

- **Track 1 — Do now, by ordinary law, inside the 1987 Constitution.** A very large share of this document needs no constitutional change: the transition-capture shield (Part 39, building on Art. 238 and the 12 March 2014 anti-corruption law), orphanage and fundraising rules (Part 40), the gang program (Part 41), skills prisons (Part 42), the technology hub (Part 43), police and army rules (Arts. 263–274 already assign frontier surveillance to the army and place the police under the Ministry of Justice — Part 44), and DR trade standards (Part 45, resting on Arts. 250–251 and 258). The Dessalines Charter (Part 37) can be adopted as a ceremonial law.
- **Track 2 — Pilot sortition as an advisory body.** A lottery-selected citizens' panel can advise Parliament, review the budget and examine laws without taking any power from it. Nothing in the 1987 text prevents advice. It builds public trust before sortition is ever load-bearing.
- **Track 3 — Constitutional change by the strict route** (Arts. 282–284), pursued with the strongest legitimacy once Tracks 1–2 have shown results. If a future consensus wants a constituent assembly instead, it must say openly that it is choosing a replacement path, not an amendment, and must be built on broad agreement, not an executive decree.

### 38.5 The reform proposals, tested

Haiti has had four serious reform efforts since 2018. The same questions were asked of each one: *Who gains power? Who funds the drafting? Does it create a single point of capture? Was the process lawful and open? Does it make some Haitians less equal? Does it create outside dependency?*

**What these efforts were:**

- **2018 — Special Committee of the Chamber of Deputies**, chaired by deputy **Jerry Tardieu**: about 30 proposals after a year of consultation. Reported ones: a presidential system, with a Vice President elected alongside the President replacing the Prime Minister; and the President answerable to ordinary courts for corruption or mismanagement in office. I could not see the full list.
- **2021 — the Moïse draft**, issued by an incumbent whose term length was itself disputed: abolish the Senate and the Prime Minister, a presidential system with a Vice President, plurality single-round presidential elections, two consecutive five-year terms, at least 5% of legislative seats for Haitians abroad, dual citizens allowed in high office, compulsory military or civic service, and amendment by legislature plus referendum.
- **2024–25 — the Constitutional Working Group (GTC)**, which Tardieu coordinated, and the **National Conference Steering Committee's draft (21 May 2025)**. Tardieu publicly argued the draft ignored the GTC's report. The draft would: eliminate the CASEC and ASEC, create departmental governors elected for five years with autonomy "outside any gaze of the central administration," concentrate executive power in a President who is also head of government, and exclude dual nationals from key offices.
- The effort to replace the Constitution was abandoned on 9 October 2025.

**Verdicts (keep / adapt / reject):**

| Proposal | Source | Verdict | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|

### 38.6 How to read any reform proposal — including this one

1. **Judge the text, the money and the process — not the author's ancestry or color.** A sound article does not become unsound because of who wrote it, and a bad article is not rescued by who wrote it. Haiti's color and class factionalism is itself a form of political capture; Dessalines' Art. 14 aimed to end it. The design answer is structural: anonymous exams, sortition, and published disparity statistics, not suspicion by ancestry.
2. **Ask who funded the drafting.** I found no public evidence either way about funding of the GTC or the Steering Committee. That is the question to put to every drafting body, and its answer should be published.
3. **Ask whether it adds a single point of capture** (a super-president, an unsupervised governor, a unanimous-consent veto).
4. **Ask whether the process is lawful and open** under Arts. 282–284.
5. **Ask whether any Haitian becomes legally lesser** than another.
6. **Ask whether it makes Haiti depend on an outside actor** for money, force or legitimacy.

- Whether the February 2026 "relaunch" of the constitution effort, reported by one source, actually happened.
- The exact timeline for an amendment under the strict route (my reading above).

## PART 39 — THE TRANSITION-CAPTURE SHIELD: THE GAP BETWEEN SELECTION AND SWEARING-IN

### 39.1 The attack, described plainly

The most effective way to capture an official is not to bribe them in office. It is to **own them before they hold office.** The pattern, as described for Haiti: right after a person is elected, drawn or named, private interests call them and say, "You are an official now; you cannot keep living in that house." Then come the hotel suite (at a top hotel), a driver at their disposal, an office in the hotel, cash, sex workers, and — for the home commune — bags of rice delivered in the official's name. No law has been voted yet. The official has not even been sworn in. But when the official arrives in office, they **already owe** the people who housed, moved, fed and compromised them, and they vote accordingly.

This threat is *larger* in this design than in an election system, for two reasons: (1) sortition-drawn members are ordinary citizens who may be poor and suddenly exposed; and (2) the system's strength — nobody can be bought before the lottery — means attackers will concentrate on the gap **after** the draw. The rule that follows: **fill the vacuum with the state before a private party can fill it.**

### 39.2 Who is covered, and from when

**Covered persons:** members of the Citizens' Assembly; the Executive Council; ministers and directors-general; judges at nomination; the Governor and Deputy Governors of the Central Bank; the heads of the DSI, customs, procurement and the tax authority; the Commander-in-Chief and the PNH Director-General; and, during the 2026–27 election cycle, **any person elected to a national or local office from the day results are proclaimed.**

**From when:** "Day 0" is the moment of the draw, nomination or proclamation. "Day 1" is the swearing-in. The shield applies from Day 0 until at least 12 months after leaving office (with the longer cooling-off in 39.9).

### 39.3 The Day-0 package: the state supplies every need a donor would

| What the attacker offers | What the state provides from Day 0 |
|---|---|
| Hotel room, villa, rental | **A choice of state-managed housing:** an apartment or house from the public housing authority, assigned by lottery among equivalent units, never a private hotel and never one chosen by a third party. No one is **required** to move: those who keep their own home keep it, with a state patrol for protection. |
| Driver and car | A **public motor pool.** Drivers are assigned by lot each week, and vehicles carry GPS records on the ledger. |
| Office in a luxury hotel | A **public office** in the Sovereign House (Part 3), with staff drawn from the NASA civil-service roster, never recruited or paid by an outside interest. |
| Bodyguards | A **rotating public protection detail** from a vetted unit (Part 44), never chosen by the official. Real protection matters: gang threats are a genuine reason an official might accept private security. |
| Cash, "loans," "advances" | **Salary starts on Day 0**, not on Day 1, plus a one-time **transition grant** that covers lost income and moving costs. |
| Rice and relief bags for the home commune | A **Commune Request Desk** (39.8): the official files a request on the public ledger, and the state delivers relief in the state's name. |
| Medical care for the official or family | State health coverage for the official and immediate family from Day 0 (Part 31). |
| A job afterward | A **return-to-work guarantee** plus **12 months of bridge pay** after the term ends (39.9). |

### 39.4 The Orientation Retreat (also Part 16)

For the 14 days between draw and swearing-in, members live at a neutral state facility, with their families housed nearby at state expense. Content:

- law, procedure and the budget;
- a **plain-language walk-through of the capture playbook**, using this very script: the hotel, the car, the cash, the women, the rice. Members hear it described by people who have seen it, before it happens to them, as a form of inoculation;
- how to report an offer, what protections follow, and what happens to people who don't;
- a physical meeting with the **Integrity Desk** (39.7), so the reporting channel is a face, not a form.

The window between draw and swearing-in is deliberately kept to **14 days or fewer.** The shorter the gap, the less room for capture.

### 39.5 The rules from Day 0

1. **Zero-gift rule.** Covered persons and their immediate family may not accept any gift, service, loan, lodging, transport, meal beyond a published low-value list, employment, sex, or donation in their name from any person or company with an interest in a state decision. Ordinary hospitality has a published value ceiling.
2. **The *offer* is reportable.** Every offer must be reported to the Integrity Desk within 24 hours, whether or not accepted. The offer is recorded in an **Offer Register**.
3. **Self-disclosure amnesty window.** Anything accepted before Day 0 or inside the first 72 hours, if reported within that window, is treated as an attempted capture of a victim, not misconduct. After the window, non-disclosure is presumed corruption. The point: a secret has no power once it is disclosed.
4. **Compromise insulation.** Private lawful conduct is not punished, and nobody is removed for being set up. The offences are *providing* a benefit as an interested party and *concealing* it. A person who is placed in a compromising situation and reports it within 72 hours is protected as a victim; the organizers are prosecuted (and, where a person was provided under coercion, trafficking law applies). This removes blackmail's fuel — the secret.
5. **Contact log.** Meetings with business representatives who have a matter before the state take place in a public meeting room at the Sovereign House, with two note-takers, and the log (who, when, what topic) is public. A meeting outside the room is a violation. Contact is not banned; it is moved into daylight. A **Public Consultation Register** gives businesses and associations a legitimate channel to submit positions so they do not need a private one.
6. **Balanced hearings.** For any bill on a sector, the Assembly hears the affected sides (importers and producers, for example) chosen by lot from registered stakeholder groups.
7. **Disclosure.** Assets, accounts, property, foreign residence or citizenship, company interests and family business ties are declared at Day 0 (an upgrade of 1987 Art. 238, which allows 30 days after entering service), published on the ledger, and verified against third-party records.

### 39.6 Hitting the giver: the demand side

The giver is the lever, because there are fewer of them than takers and they have more to lose.

- **Crime of capture attempt.** Offering or providing a prohibited benefit to a covered person is a criminal offence, the same whether accepted or not.
- **Corporate debarment.** The firm and its beneficial owners are barred from public contracts, concessions, licenses and import permits for **10 years**, via the beneficial-owner register on the ledger (Part 46).
- **Forfeiture and fine** scaled to the firm's turnover, with a statutory floor.
- **The benefit is forfeited, and so is the prize.** A law, contract or decision that passed with the vote of a member who accepted an *undisclosed* prohibited benefit is **voidable** in court for a defined period, and the beneficiary must **disgorge** the gains. The buyer cannot keep what he bought. This is the single strongest deterrent in this Part.
- **Executives are personally liable**, and the case is published.
- **Consequences are non-retroactive:** a rule applies only to benefits offered after it takes effect.

### 39.7 Detection: how the state finds out

No design can make bribery impossible. It can make it **detectable, expensive and unprofitable.** Sources of detection:

1. **The covered person's own report** (protected, rewarded with an integrity record).
2. **Hotel and car-rental reporting rule.** Any hotel, guesthouse, short-let platform or car-rental company must report to the Integrity Desk within 48 hours any booking or rental for a covered person that is paid by a third party. Failure puts the business license at risk. This targets the hotel-suite tactic directly.
3. **Payment signals.** Payments to covered persons and immediate family are flagged on the national payment rail (Part 32) and by the financial intelligence unit (UCREF). Third-party payments of rent, school fees or medical bills are caught.
4. **Whistleblowers**, including hotel staff, drivers, bank employees and members' own staff, with a protected hotline and protection from retaliation.
5. **Integrity tests.** Random, court-authorized tests in which an undercover agent makes a standard offer. Safeguards against entrapment: the offer cannot be more tempting than real ones, the tests are random across all covered persons, the results are logged, and the **Integrity Desk's own staff are tested as well.**
6. **"Who benefits" report.** Fourteen days before any vote on a sector in a high-risk list (customs, import licensing, concessions, mining, energy, telecom), the Integrity Desk publishes the contact logs and ownership of the beneficiaries. Votes in those sectors are by recorded roll call.

**The Integrity Desk** sits under the DSI Oversight Commission (Part 25.2), not under the Executive. Its staff are rotated, are themselves covered persons, and are audited by the Superior Court of Auditors (CSCCA). Its findings go to the courts, not to a minister.

### 39.8 The home-commune "rice bag" channel

An official's commune will need help. The shield does not leave the community without it; it keeps the *credit* out of private hands.

- A **Commune Request Desk** takes an official's request for relief; the state delivers it in the state's name, and the delivery is posted to the ledger.
- Private giving to a commune where a sitting official is from must go through the commune's **public account**, be posted to the ledger, and **may not be announced in the official's name or in connection with them.**

### 39.9 The exit: ending the "deferred bribe"

The same attackers who cannot buy an official in office can promise a job later. The shield treats the end of the term as seriously as the start.

- **Bridge pay and return-to-work.** Members get 12 months of bridge pay and a right to return to their previous employment or a comparable state placement. A person with a safe landing has less reason to sell the vote.
- **Cooling-off.** A former member of the Assembly or the Executive Council, or a minister, may not take a job with, consult for, or lobby for a firm or sector that benefited from a decision they voted on or administered: **2 years** for Assembly members, **3 years** for the Executive Council, ministers and heads of regulators. Because bridge pay compensates for the sacrifice, the rule can be strict.
- **Retaliation protection.** Officials who vote against powerful interests are threatened as well as bribed. Protective detail extends through the term and for a defined period afterward.

### 39.10 Staff, relatives and the nominee problem

Capture often goes through the people around the official. The Day-0 rules apply to:

- **Immediate family** (spouse, children, parents, siblings), linked to Part 14's narrow complicity standard: no guilt by association, but family benefits are covered by the gift ban.
- **Personal staff.** The official's staff are drawn from the NASA civil-service roster and rotated; nobody is paid by a private party.
- **Nominees and fronts.** Using a relative, friend or company to receive a benefit is treated as receiving it.

### 39.11 The elected-official transition (now, inside the 1987 Constitution)

This Part does not need to wait for sortition. For the **2026–27 election cycle**, the Prime Minister's government, parliament and the electoral authorities can adopt the core of the shield by ordinary law: a gift ban from the proclamation of results; disclosure under Art. 238, moved to proclamation day; hotel and car-rental reporting; and corporate debarment under the 2014 anti-corruption law, which the IMF's 2025 governance diagnostic describes as outdated and in need of renewal. State housing and an official-vehicle pool for newly elected officials are cheap relative to a single captured procurement decision.

### 39.12 Entrenchment

The following are added to the entrenched clauses in Part 19.1, because they are exactly what a captured Assembly would try to weaken: **the zero-gift rule, the giver's debarment and disgorgement, the Offer Register, and the independence of the Integrity Desk.**

### 39.13 Honest limits

- It depends on the judiciary. If the courts are captured, debarment and disgorgement are empty threats (Part 2).
- It cannot remove physical danger. If protection is weak, private security will keep tempting officials.
- Integrity tests and state-supplied housing create their own surveillance risks. The safeguards are judicial authorization, randomness, publication, and the fact that the Integrity Desk is tested too.
- The cost is unknown (the page's open-decisions log already notes there is no cost model). It is small per official; whether Haiti can fund it reliably is a budget fact to be tested, not assumed.

## PART 40 — CHILDREN FIRST: ORPHANAGES, FOREIGN ABUSERS, AND THE DIGNITY OF HAITIAN CHILDREN

This Part strengthens Part 35 and **supersedes** its 35.2 (the blanket ban on foreign-funded orphanages) and 35.4's second bullet (routing all child fundraising through the treasury), for the reasons in 40.7.

### 40.1 What the evidence shows

- **Scale.** About 750 orphanages and roughly 30,000 children in them (Lumos Foundation). The Government of Haiti estimates that **about 80% of these children have at least one living parent.** Only about 15% of the orphanages were registered.
- **Money.** The Lumos report found that over 90% of the orphanage funders it identified were from the United States, about 90% were faith-based, and total funding exceeded US$100 million a year. Money paid per child makes each child a source of income — a built-in reason to recruit children.
- **Quality.** The state child-protection agency (IBESR) inspected 754 orphanages in 2018 (nearly 26,000 children). Only 35 met minimum standards, close to 400 were ordered closed, and inspectors recorded physical or sexual abuse in more than three-quarters, according to reporting in the *Christian Science Monitor*. The government banned new orphanages that year. IBESR staff have gone months without pay, and staff trying to close orphanages have been threatened.
- **Documented foreign abusers.** Two US-based founders of Haitian children's homes have been convicted in US federal court under child-sex-tourism laws for crimes committed in Haiti: **Daniel Pye**, who ran an orphanage in Jacmel from 2006 to 2012 (convicted November 2017, sentenced to 40 years), and **Michael Geilenfeld**, founder of St. Joseph's Home for Boys (convicted February 2025, sentenced to 210 years; the counts cover 2005–2010). In both cases the abuser's position as operator gave regular access to the children, funding came through religious and non-profit networks abroad, and the **prosecution happened in the United States, years later.** Haitian victims were dependent on a foreign justice system for justice.
- **Displacement.** The UN estimates about 1.5 million people are displaced, more than half of them children, and child recruitment by armed groups has risen sharply. Closing institutions without somewhere safe to place children would do harm (40.5).

### 40.2 The principle

**A child is not a source of income, and poverty is never a reason to separate a child from a family.** The order of response stays as in Part 35.1: keep the child with the family; help the family; kinship and foster care; small community-based care; large residential care only as a court-ordered last resort.

### 40.3 One register, one license, one inspector with no friends

1. **National Child Register.** Every child in any residential setting is recorded (civil-registry identity, family links, the court order that placed them, and the date of every review) in a protected, access-controlled system. Child records are never public on the ledger; only institution-level statistics are.
2. **A license for every facility** — state-run included — renewed annually, with unannounced inspections by IBESR, the Protector of Citizens (Art. 207) and **two randomly drawn citizen observers** (the same sortition logic as the Assembly). Inspection results are published.
3. **Funding follows outcomes, not beds.** Foreign or private money for residential care goes into a **registered Haitian account** posted on the ledger, and may not be paid per child housed. Funding rewards reunification, foster placement and school attendance.
4. **Haitian-majority governance.** A foreign-founded or foreign-funded facility must be a Haitian legal entity with a Haitian-majority board, an annual independent audit, liability insurance and **personal liability for its directors.** The foreign **sponsor** (church, charity or company) is disclosed and is **jointly liable** for abuses at the facility it funds.
5. **Staff pay and safety for the inspectors.** IBESR's inspectors are paid on time, protected, and rotated; threats against them are prosecuted as obstruction.

### 40.4 Child-safe presence: the rule for every adult near a child

The rule applies to **every adult, whatever their nationality, religion or role** — Haitian or foreign, clergy or lay, volunteer or founder. What it closes is the combination that enabled the convicted cases: **unvetted access + independent fundraising + no oversight.**

- **Child-Safe Register.** Nobody works, volunteers or lives in a place with children in residential care without being on it. To be listed: police clearance from every country of residence in the last ten years, authenticated; a check against available sex-offender and child-protection records in those countries; the Haitian integrity screening of Part 17; safeguarding training; and IBESR approval.
- **Two-adult rule, no private rooms, no overnight stays with children, no unsupervised outings.** Permanent staff and founders included.
- **No orphanage tourism and no short-term care volunteering.** Short-term visitors and mission groups may not care for, host or photograph children in residential settings. Volunteers are welcome in community, school-building, health, skills and infrastructure roles under a licensed sponsor. This follows the position of child-protection agencies that short visits harm children and fuel the market for orphanages.
- **Faith-based and other charities are welcome on the same terms.** The rules protect children; they say nothing about belief.
- **Loss of listing is shared.** Removal from the Register is notified, within 48 hours, to the child-protection and law-enforcement authorities of the person's home country, and to the person's sponsoring organization.

### 40.5 Closing the bad institutions without hurting children

Mass closure without placements would put children on the street, into the hands of armed groups, or into *restavek* households. The sequence is therefore fixed:

1. **Month 0–6:** register every facility and every child; identify the worst (Lumos estimates at least 140 with extremely detrimental conditions); begin family tracing for every child.
2. **Month 6–18:** close the worst first, with a child-by-child placement plan signed by IBESR before any closure; emergency foster care for children who cannot go home safely.
3. **In parallel:** **family preservation.** Cash support, school fees, health coverage and parenting support for the families that would otherwise place a child, funded from the budget (Parts 5.4, 5.5, 23) and from the funds that used to go to beds. If a family could keep its child with a fraction of what an institution costs, the state should pay the fraction.
4. **Foster care law.** A national foster-care framework with licensing, monitoring and the same Child-Safe Register, designed explicitly so that a foster placement cannot turn into unpaid domestic labor (the *restavek* risk, which has been unlawful since 2003 but persists). Any placement is checked at school-enrollment verification and in home visits.
5. **Shrink to last resort:** a small number of well-run, small-scale residential homes, by court order only.

**Adoption** is a last resort for a child who cannot be raised in Haiti, goes through the central authority only, never through an orphanage or a private agent, and follows an international-adoption framework with strict anti-trafficking checks.

### 40.6 Prosecuting abusers, whatever their passport

- **Haitian jurisdiction first.** Crimes against children in Haiti are tried in Haitian courts regardless of the accused's nationality. The Child Protection Brigade (BPM) and the anti-trafficking unit (Part 35.3) are the lead investigators.
- **A cooperation file for every case.** When a suspect leaves Haiti, or when a foreign prosecution is possible (as in the Pye and Geilenfeld cases, brought under US laws against travel for illicit sexual conduct), Haiti hands over its evidence and **keeps the victim at the center**: the **Victim Justice Fund** pays for travel, protection, counseling and compensation, so a Haitian child's access to justice does not depend on charity.
- **Information sharing.** Convictions and Child-Safe Register removals are shared with partner states and flagged for Interpol notices where applicable (Part 5.8 GLDD).
- **Sponsor accountability.** Funding organizations that ignored warnings are civilly liable and are removed from the registry.

### 40.7 The Children's Dignity Code: regulating the *appeal*, not the speech

**What it covers.** How organizations **ask for money** using children, and how children in vulnerable situations may be shown for that purpose. It applies equally to foreign and Haitian organizations, to charities, churches, companies and individual fundraisers, and to anything online or offline.

**What it does not cover — and why this is a hard line.** The state does not review, approve, license, filter or take down **reporting, opinion, photography of public events, or criticism of Haiti or its government** — by Haitians or foreigners. Reasons:

1. **It is already entrenched.** Part 6 guarantees no prior restraint and ordinary-court-only defamation; Part 19.1 entrenches press freedom. A state power to control what is said about the country would sit in the hands of whoever holds office next, and the first thing it would be used for is hiding corruption.
2. **The abuse cases above were exposed by testimony and reporting.** Hiding suffering also hides the abusers and starves the victims of help.
3. **Silence is not a reputation.** What changes how Haiti is seen is what Haiti builds and says.

**The rules:**

1. **No fundraising images of children in residential care, state custody, or medical or crisis distress, and no images of survivors of abuse or trafficking,** without informed consent of the child (age-appropriate) and the guardian or IBESR, and a dignity review. For institutional residents the default is a ban, except fully anonymized material.
2. **Honest appeals.** A fundraiser must say how much of each gift reaches the beneficiary and may not imply a result the money does not buy (for example, "sponsor this child" when the money goes to a general fund). A misleading appeal is fraud.
3. **Registered fundraisers.** Any organization soliciting for Haitian children registers: identity, board, accounts, audited accounts and beneficiaries, and posts its spending to the ledger. Foreign organizations are welcome; unregistered ones may not use children's images or appeal in Haiti's name.
4. **Right to withdraw and be forgotten.** A child, or the adult they become, may demand removal of their image and story.
5. **Storytelling standard.** The person has a voice and a name they choose; solutions are shown alongside need; Haitian people tell Haitian stories.
6. **Penalties** are administrative and civil — registry removal, fines, disgorgement of money raised through a violating appeal, and a request to payment processors and platforms to apply their own fraud rules — plus criminal law for fraud and child sexual exploitation material. **Never** for journalism, personal posts or opinion.

**Why Part 35.4's second bullet is replaced.** Routing every child-related donation through the treasury would make the treasury the biggest honey pot for capture, cut off honest private giving, and invite exactly the corruption the rest of this document works against. Registration, audit and publication give the same protection with less risk.

### 40.8 Telling Haiti's story: a better answer than a takedown

- **A Haitian Storytelling Fund**: grants to Haitian filmmakers, journalists, creators and photographers; training for young Haitians in digital media; support for diaspora creators.
- **A Haiti Data Desk** that publishes verified facts — not takedown requests — on health, schools, harvests, universities, startups and security gains, and responds to false claims with evidence.
- **Right of reply for false facts** through the ordinary civil courts (defamation as in Part 6).
- **Cultural and tourism promotion** (Part 5.7) and the technology hub (Part 43) as the show window of what Haiti builds.

### 40.9 Metrics (published on the ledger)

Children in residential care (target: falling every quarter); share reunified or in family-based care; facilities licensed and inspected; inspections with findings and the follow-up; Child-Safe Register entries and removals; abuse reports and outcomes; and time from complaint to charge.

### 40.10 Honest limits

- Without a functioning IBESR, none of this works; fund and protect it first.
- Gang territory and displacement make family tracing slow and reunification sometimes unsafe.
- Families need real support, or placement will continue "by choice."
- Foster care that is not monitored can become another route to exploitation.
- Foreign prosecution depends on foreign cooperation; Haiti's own courts must be able to do the job (Part 2).

## PART 41 — ARMED GROUPS: JUDGMENT, DISARMAMENT AND REINTEGRATION

### 41.1 The starting point (mid-2026)

- Armed groups control most of Port-au-Prince and spread into Artibonite and Centre. In the first quarter of 2026 the UN recorded 1,642 people killed and 745 injured; sexual violence against at least 292 people; about 1.47 million people displaced. The UN recorded 892 children recruited by armed groups in 2025.
- The same UN reporting says **69% of casualties in January–March 2026 came from security-force operations or police summary executions.** A disarmament program that is mostly killing, with no judgment, becomes a recruitment tool for the next group. The judgment track below is a security measure, not a kindness.
- In May 2026 the UN Secretary-General offered three disarmament-demobilization-reintegration options for Haiti: rapid (about US$13.5 million a year), comprehensive (about US$34 million a year) and multi-year (about US$100–150 million), and recommended the multi-year option with a trust fund co-chaired by Haiti and the UN. Haiti has also tried disarmament commissions before; I did not re-verify the detail of earlier programs, but the pattern that groups re-form after a short program is the lesson to design against.

### 41.2 The principle: surrender is better than war, and **nobody who commits grave crimes escapes judgment**

Three doors are open to every member of an armed group, each with a deadline.

**Door 1 — Children (under 18).** Treated as victims. No prosecution for what the group made them do. Immediate transfer to child-protection centers (Part 40), family tracing, health care, school or trade training, psychosocial care, and protection from reprisals. A child is never held in an adult prison.

**Door 2 — Adults who surrender, with or without weapons.** Each is seen by an individual **Reintegration Panel**: a judge, a prosecutor, a trained social worker, a victims' advocate and a **commune delegate drawn by lot** from the area affected. The panel sorts the person into one of three tracks:

| Track | Who | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **A — Low involvement** | Coerced, support roles, no grave crimes | Conditional release into the Reintegration Program (41.4) with monitoring |
| **B — Serious offences, not grave** | Armed robbery, extortion, assaults | Reduced custodial sentence served in a **skills prison** (Part 42), restitution, then supervised release |
| **C — Grave crimes** | Murder, massacre, mass sexual violence, kidnapping leadership, trafficking, recruitment of children | **No amnesty.** Ordinary trial. Reduced sentence available only for full confession, handing over weapons and evidence, and naming financiers and suppliers |

International law limits amnesty for the gravest crimes, and in Haiti's case it would also produce the impunity that made groups strong in the first place. The credible offer is *not* "forgiveness"; it is "a fair hearing, a defined outcome, and a path out."

**Door 3 — The deadline passes.** After the published window closes, people who keep their weapons are arrested under normal law by the police, with the GSF in support. **Arrest comes first.** Force is used under published rules of engagement, with body cameras, immediate review of every death by an independent unit, and criminal accountability for summary killings (Parts 14, 33.3). The statistic above is why.

### 41.3 Cut the supply: weapons and money

Disarming fighters while the financiers and suppliers remain is a revolving door.

- **Financier window.** Business people, politicians and officials who funded or armed groups may come forward, cooperate and receive reduced sentences. After the window closes, the ledger, the beneficial-owner register (Part 46) and the financial intelligence unit are used to prosecute those who didn't. Their assets fund the **Victims' Reparations Fund.** UN sanctions listings already exist for some individuals; Haiti should feed them evidence.
- **Arms tracing.** Every surrendered weapon is logged by serial number and traced to its source. Surrender is **not paid in cash per gun** (that can create a market for weapons); a surrendering area earns a **community project** (road, school, clinic, water) instead.
- **Border and port controls** (Parts 29, 45): arms and ammunition seizures are tracked on the ledger.

### 41.4 The Reintegration Program

- **Where.** Away from the former group's territory for the first months, then supervised return, with a **community acceptance council** (residents, victims' advocates, elders, faith leaders) consulted before any return.
- **Income.** A 12–24 month stipend paid into an account on the national payment rail (Part 32), never in cash from an armed group's former patron, at or below a comparable apprentice wage.
- **Skills.** A certified trade (the National Skills Guarantee of Part 37, Art. 11): construction, solar installation, welding, carpentry, mechanics, agriculture, tailoring, digital skills. The **technology hub's second-chance track** (Part 43) is open to those with the aptitude.
- **Equity rule.** For every reintegrated person, the commune's non-armed youth get at least as many training places. Otherwise the program pays people to be dangerous and angers everyone who stayed out of it.
- **Security.** Protection from reprisal by former leaders, and from vigilante killing; witnesses and cooperating defendants get protection.
- **Relapse.** A returned fighter is sent back to the judgment track; there are no repeat Door-2 hearings.
- **Women and girls.** Separate care and a protected track for those held by groups (many are survivors of sexual violence), not treated as "associates."

### 41.5 Victims and truth

- **Victims' Reparations Fund**, from seized assets and convicted leaders' property.
- **Victim participation.** A victims' advocate sits on every panel; victims are informed and heard in every case.
- **Truth Hearings.** Cooperating defendants give public testimony on who ordered, funded and armed their groups. Colombia's transitional-justice model (reduced restorative sanctions in return for full truth, with no amnesty for the gravest crimes) is the reference point, adapted rather than copied.

### 41.6 Governance and sovereignty

- **A Haitian Reintegration Authority** under the Ministry of Justice & Public Security, with its budget and every case outcome on the ledger.
- **Donor money in, donor command out.** Donors fund a trust fund chaired by Haitian authorities; foreign staff and contractors do not run the operational chain or hold Haitian data (Part 44). The UN's role is financing and technical support.
- **Capture rules apply.** Panel members, program staff and contractors are covered persons for gift and contact rules (Part 39). A program with this much money and this many powerful former fighters is itself a target.

### 41.7 Honest limits

- The program cannot work where armed groups win more by fighting than by surrendering. It needs the security effort to change the odds and the money flows to be cut at the same time.
- It needs a working court system. Without one, panels cannot judge anyone.
- The costs are the UN's estimates, not a Haitian cost model, which this document still lacks.
- Leaders with power and money will not surrender; the program is mainly for the rank and file, and for creating defections that weaken the leadership.

## PART 42 — SKILLS PRISONS: JAILS THAT SEND PEOPLE HOME WITH A TRADE

### 42.1 The starting point

- About **7,160** people were in Haitian prisons in mid-2025, in a system built for about 3,900 (at least 300% of capacity in early 2024). **82% were awaiting trial**; some had waited years. 52 prisoners died in one quarter of 2025, mostly of preventable causes (poor food, water, health care). The UN has reported that money meant for food is diverted by corruption. In March 2024 armed groups attacked the two main prisons in Port-au-Prince and about 4,600 inmates escaped, many reportedly rejoining groups.
- The 1987 Constitution already guarantees the right to be brought before a judge within 48 hours (Art. 26), to counsel (Arts. 24-3, 25-1) and bans unnecessary force or brutality (Art. 25). The gap is practice, not text.
- Dessalines' Art. 11 — "every citizen must possess a mechanic art" — is the founding idea: a Haitian leaves the state's care with a trade.

### 42.2 Rule 1: first, empty the cells that should not be full

A skills prison cannot work if 82% of its population is unconvicted. Before and alongside construction:

- **Pretrial limit.** A maximum period for pretrial detention by offense category (to be fixed by law), after which release with conditions is automatic unless a judge issues a reasoned order.
- **Hearings inside prisons** (as UN-backed judges have started to do) to clear the backlog, with the National Public Defender Service (Part 2).
- **Non-custodial options** for minor offences: community service, supervised release, restitution.
- **Strict separation:** untried from convicted, women from men, children from adults (children are never held with adults).

### 42.3 Rule 2: the skills prison, design

1. **Small regional prisons** close to families (and courts), not mega-prisons. Research in several countries links regular family contact to lower re-offending, and visiting is far harder when the only prison is days away.
2. **Risk classification** at entry. People convicted of grave violent crime or gang leadership are held in a separate **high-security unit** with controlled communications, so leaders cannot run networks or recruit inside. They still receive education and health care.
3. **Education first:** literacy in Kreyòl and French, then school equivalency.
4. **Trades with real certificates.** Construction trades, solar installation, electrical work, welding, carpentry, plumbing, auto and motorcycle repair, agriculture and food processing, tailoring, baking, and digital skills. Exams are the **same national skills exams** as outside, and the certificate does not mention prison.
5. **Real work, real wages.** Prisoners are paid a fair rate. Part goes to family and victim restitution, part to a **savings account released at exit.** Wages are paid through the national payment rail (Part 32) and posted to the ledger in aggregate.
6. **Output goes to the public.** Prison workshops make school desks, hospital beds, building elements for public housing (Part 34), and agricultural tools, sold only to public bodies through open procurement.
7. **Release pathway.** A pre-release unit 6–12 months before exit; a placement officer; a first-job guarantee with public works or a licensed employer; and a mentor from the community.

### 42.4 Rule 3: keep the prison from becoming a business

Prisons attract capture: food contracts, supply deals, cheap labor. Rules:

- **No private firm leases prison labor.** Prisoners work for public institutions only, never for a private company's profit (that is how forced labor re-enters through the back door).
- **Food and medical purchases** are procured openly (Part 24), with publicly posted menus, quantities and delivery records. Every meal's unit cost is published.
- **Staff pay and rotation.** Guards are paid enough to resist smuggling bribes, rotated between units, vetted under Part 17, and are covered by the Part 39 gift rules.
- **Body-scan and search protocols**, cameras, and a complaint line that goes directly to a judge and to the Protector of Citizens.
- **Unannounced inspections** by the Protector of Citizens (Art. 207) and two randomly drawn citizen observers, as in Part 40, with findings published. Death in custody triggers an automatic independent investigation.

### 42.5 Rule 4: security, because the last escape was catastrophic

- Perimeter and evacuation plans, with the army's support role (Part 44) limited to external defense of the facility, not running it.
- High-risk prisoners are held where escape is hardest, away from Port-au-Prince-style choke points.
- Records are held off-site and mirrored so a breach cannot erase sentences or identities (the logic of Part 8).

### 42.6 Measures (published)

Pretrial share of the prison population (target: falling every quarter); occupancy; deaths in custody; trade certificates earned; employment and re-arrest at 12 and 24 months after release; cost per prisoner; and the number of complaints and their outcomes.

### 42.7 Honest limits

- There is nowhere safe to build in the areas where armed groups are strongest. The rollout follows the security map (Part 18.4).
- It costs money, which this document has not yet costed. Release of the untried first reduces cost faster than construction.
- Trades only work if jobs exist outside: that is why the housing program (Part 34), the technology hub (Part 43) and the public-works pipeline are the other half of this idea.

## PART 43 — THE NATIONAL TECHNOLOGY HUB: WHERE YOUNG HAITIANS BUILD AND LAUNCH

### 43.1 Purpose

A place where young Haitians — in Haiti and abroad — can build products, found companies and reach customers, without needing a connected family, a foreign sponsor or a bribe. It does three jobs: creates skilled work, builds Haitian-owned capacity in payments, farming, health, education, energy and civic tools, and gives the skills prisons and reintegration program (Parts 41–42) a high-end destination. It is the Dessalines "trade for every citizen" at the top of the ladder.

### 43.2 Shape

- **One flagship campus** in a city chosen by published criteria (security, reliable power, fibre or submarine-cable access, an airport, a university nearby, affordable housing). Which city is a decision for the Assembly with public hearings; **not** decided by a minister.
- **Ten département labs**, small and low-cost, run through the Sovereign Houses (Part 3), so a student in Fort-Liberté or Jérémie has the same tools and mentors on a screen.
- **A diaspora bridge:** a seat on the board and a mentor network for Haitian founders in North America, Europe and the Caribbean, with the Brain-Gain Fund (Part 30).

### 43.3 What it offers

| Service | Detail |
|---|---|
| Space, power, connectivity | Solar plus battery microgrid; redundant internet; shared workshops and testing hardware |
| Company set-up | Registration, tax number and bank account in one day, through the one-stop registry |
| Money | Small grants in **tranches tied to milestones**; a **co-investment fund** that invests only alongside private investors, never alone (the Israeli "Yozma" model of the 1990s — state money that reduces the risk for private venture funds rather than picking winners) |
| Mentors and training | Experienced founders, engineers and lawyers; coding schools open to graduates of the skills prisons and reintegration program |
| Payments and data | Sandbox access to the national payment rail (Part 32) and open civic data so startups can build on it |
| Market access | Public-sector pilots through open procurement (Part 24), with a ceiling so a startup is not dependent on one government buyer |
| Priority sectors | Farming and cold chain, solar and microgrids, payments and microfinance, health and telemedicine, education in Kreyòl, logistics, construction technology, civic transparency tools |

### 43.4 Fair access: how entries are chosen

- **Open competition, blind review.** Applications are scored without names or photos (the NASA principle of Part 17); the panel includes experts, diaspora founders and **randomly drawn citizen jurors.** This blocks the usual route where connected families get the places.
- **Age 18–35 for the core program**, plus a second-chance track.
- **Published results.** The list of teams funded, the amounts, and the scores go on the ledger.

### 43.5 Anti-capture rules

1. **No equity for officials.** No minister, Assembly member, hub staff member or their families may own a share in a hub company, advise one for pay, or sit on a selection panel for a company they know.
2. **Founders keep their intellectual property.** The hub takes no IP. Public-benefit code for civic tools is open-source.
3. **Foreign money allowed, foreign control not.** Foreign investors may hold minority stakes. Funding from any foreign government or its agencies is disclosed on the ledger and cannot carry a board seat, data access or approval rights.
4. **Data stays in Haiti.** Citizen data and the national ledger are not hosted on a foreign-government-controlled cloud. A company may use foreign cloud services for its own products as the law allows.
5. **A Haitian-control floor** (for example, 51% founder or Haitian ownership during the first five years of subsidies) is listed as an **open decision**, because a floor protects Haitian ownership but can deter investors.
6. **Open procurement for the hub itself,** with beneficial-owner disclosure (Part 46) and the debarment rule of Part 39 if a contractor tries to buy the project.
7. **Board by lot plus founders.** The hub board is drawn by sortition from a pool of registered founders, engineers and educators, with a diaspora seat; the director serves a fixed non-renewable term.

### 43.6 Tax and trade

A time-limited profit-tax holiday and duty-free equipment import for hub companies, with anti-abuse rules (a company that moves its profits or sells its equipment loses the benefit). The hub is **not** an offshore enclave: companies pay Haitian payroll and social contributions.

### 43.7 Measures and honest limits

- Published: companies started and surviving at 1, 3 and 5 years; jobs created; revenue; share of founders from outside the capital; women's share; skills-prison and reintegration graduates placed; amount of public money in and private money mobilized.
- **Risks:** unreliable power and internet; a vanity project that picks a favorite city; brain drain to the very countries that train Haitian founders; the hub becoming a government vendor list. The anti-capture rules above are the design response, not a guarantee.
- **Cost:** not yet estimated; the funding sources are a budget line, a diaspora matching fund, and donor grants **without governance rights.**

## PART 44 — THE SOVEREIGN PUBLIC FORCE: NO FOREIGN COMMAND, A POLICE THAT FITS THE POPULATION, THE ARMY ON THE LINE

This Part revises Parts 26, 29.1, 33.2 and 33.5 where they conflict with it. The conflict is flagged where it appears.

### 44.1 The facts that shape the design

- **Army (FAd'H):** disbanded in 1995 after decades of coups and abuses, re-established in 2017. Strength is reported at roughly **1,300–1,500** personnel (mid-2025 to early 2026 sources). A 5-year plan announced in October 2024 aims at **20,000** (about 4,000 a year), with OAS-coordinated support from member states. The 2025–26 budget is about HTG 10.6 billion (about US$81 million, around 0.2% of GDP). The first battalion deployed to central Port-au-Prince on 7 April 2026; reporting says few units are deployed along the border.
- **Police (PNH):** about 13,500 officers on the books in 2025 (13,196 at the end of 2023, of whom only about 4,000 were on duty at any time). Fewer than 300 of about 570 communal sections had any police presence in mid-2025. More than 3,000 officers resigned between 2021 and 2023. The P4000 program's first cohort of 877 graduated in January 2026.
- **Foreign forces:** the UN-authorized Gang Suppression Force (up to 5,550 authorized; about 1,000 reported on the ground by mid-2026) works alongside the PNH. A private military company is reported (by UN-watchers) to be working with the PNH.
- **Foreign training and supply:** Mexico, Colombia, France, Argentina and Brazil have trained recruits; Brazil is reported to supply small arms; the United States restricts lethal aid under its Leahy Law and has provided limited non-lethal assistance; Taiwan and the UAE have been mentioned for equipment.

### 44.2 The constitution already points this way

The 1987 Constitution (as amended in 2012) already provides that:

- Only two armed corps exist, the army and the police, and "no other armed corps may exist in the national territory" (Arts. 263, 263-1).
- The army exists to "guaranty the defense and the integrity of the territory," including "the surveillance of the land, maritime and aerial frontiers," to help the police when the Executive asks, and to assist in disasters (Arts. 264, 266).
- The army has a monopoly on weapons of war (Art. 268-3); it is apolitical (Art. 265); active-duty officers need to retire two years before seeking elected office (Art. 267-1).
- The police is an armed body under the Ministry of Justice (Arts. 269–269-1).
- The President "never commands" the forces in person (Art. 143).

**So the army on the border line is not a new idea; it is the existing constitutional duty.** What this Part adds is the method and the limits.

### 44.3 The army on the Line, the civilians at the Gates (revises Parts 29.1, 33.2, 33.5)

Haiti's land border is about 380 km long. Split it into two jobs:

| | **The Line** (the boundary between crossing points) | **The Gates** (the four official crossings: Ouanaminthe, Belladère, Malpasse, Anse-à-Pitres) |
|---|---|---|
| **Who** | **The army** (a Border Command) | **Civilians:** customs, the Border Tactical Shield, migration and health staff (DG Sekirite Fwontyè, AG Douan) |
| **Job** | Surveillance, patrol, deterring incursions and armed groups, stopping arms and ammunition moving between gates, defending against attack | Inspection, tax, quarantine, identity, humanitarian reception of returnees (Part 29.4) |
| **Why** | Art. 266(3). External defense is the army's job and a presence along the whole line is what only the army can provide | **Money and people meet here.** Giving soldiers control over customs makes the army a trading and taxing power, which is how armies become political (the very danger in 33.2). So the Gates stay civilian |
| **Interface** | A person or load caught crossing between gates is stopped by the army and **handed to the nearest civilian authority within 24 hours**; the army does not investigate, charge, or hold people for longer. The 48-hour judge rule of Art. 26 applies from the time of arrest | Army and customs run a **joint, logged hotline**; every army handover is entered on the ledger |

This keeps the entrenched clause intact: **no independent domestic policing by the army** (Part 19.1). The Line is an external-defense and frontier duty (Art. 266); it is not a role in towns, markets, protests or elections.

**Border Command safeguards:** published rules of engagement; body cameras; an independent review of any death at the border; rotation of units so none becomes a local power; officers' pay and housing at the frontier funded centrally and posted on the ledger; no unit may collect fees from travelers or traders; spot audits by randomly drawn citizen observers at the Gates.

### 44.4 A police that fits the population

- **Size by formula, not by headcount.** A published coverage standard: at least a staffed station for every communal section, and officers per resident by risk level. The current plan is 19,000–20,000 (Part 26.2), about 1.6 officers per 1,000 residents at 11.9 million people. The target is reviewed every year against the actual coverage map.
- **From the communities they serve.** Recruits come from every département and commune, speak Kreyòl, and are at least **30% women** (Art. 17-1). First posting in the home département but not the home commune, with rotation every few years so no officer becomes entangled locally (the anti-isolation rule of Part 26.2).
- **Payroll integrity.** The gap between 13,196 on the books and about 4,000 on duty is where "ghost officers" and unpaid positions hide. Every salary is tied to a biometric attendance record; the payroll for each unit is on the ledger; an independent census audit counts the force each year.
- **Community policing and procedural justice** (Part 33.3) as the default posture; **use-of-force and deaths in custody** are reviewed by an independent unit (Parts 14, 41).
- **Pay that resists corruption**, funded by Haiti (44.5 rule 4).

### 44.5 The ten rules against foreign command and foreign influence

1. **Haitian command, always.** Every unit of the army and police is commanded by Haitian officers under Haitian civilian authority. Foreign personnel hold advisory and training roles only.
2. **The GSF is an ally, not a commander.** It operates under its UN mandate (Resolution 2793) *alongside* the PNH and FAd'H. The terms (status-of-forces agreement, rules of engagement, accountability for abuses, handover of detainees to Haitian courts, exit conditions) are published on the ledger. Handover to Haiti is tied to the PNH coverage map (Part 26.4). *Open question:* how Haiti's consent interacts with a Security Council mandate is a question for international lawyers.
3. **No single foreign supplier or trainer.** No country trains, equips or finances more than about a third of any cohort or equipment class, so no single government holds the spare-parts, ammunition or training supply line. Contracts are on the ledger; ammunition and spare-parts stock is held in Haiti.
4. **Haiti pays its own force.** Salaries come from the national budget. Donor money may fund equipment and training, but **salaries and promotions are never paid or decided by a foreign party**, because whoever pays decides.
5. **Selection for foreign courses is by blind merit and rotation,** not by a foreign embassy's preference. Trainees disclose all per-diems, scholarships and gifts. A foreign government may not pay a Haitian officer personally.
6. **One channel for foreign intelligence,** run by the DSI under the Oversight Commission (Part 25.2), logged and minuted. No foreign service has direct access to Haitian databases, the biometric registry or the ledger.
7. **Contractors on a short leash.** A private military or security company may be used only in a declared emergency, by public contract on the ledger, under Haitian command and Haitian law, with a time limit, no immunity, and a Haitian officer present at any armed operation. Their abuses are tried in Haitian courts.
8. **Leverage disclosure and the role rule.** Commanders, the heads of intelligence, customs and the police, and judges of the Constitutional Court hold **no foreign citizenship or residence** while in office; they and their families disclose all foreign assets and accounts (Part 38.5). A visa is a leash; this removes the leash from the posts where it would do most harm.
9. **No foreign base** or permanent foreign garrison without a two-thirds Assembly vote *and* a referendum, and no secret annex to any agreement.
10. **Entrenched.** Rules 1, 4, 7 and 9, plus the civilian-control rule "the armed force is essentially obedient; no armed body can deliberate" (Dessalines, General Art. 4), are added to the entrenched clauses (Part 19.1).

### 44.6 Honest limits

- A 20,000-strong army and a 19,000-strong police are expensive; there is no cost model in this document.
- Haiti cannot, in the short run, equip and train a force without foreign help. The rules above reduce dependency and make it visible; they do not abolish it.
- A larger army is a larger risk. The history (the 1995 disbanding) is why the Line/Gate split, the Defense Nominating Council (Part 33.2) and the entrenched limits are built in before the army grows, not after.

## PART 45 — THE DOMINICAN REPUBLIC AND THE WORLD: A STRICT GATE ON GOODS, AND A COST FOR DISRESPECT

This Part extends Part 29 and Part 30. It keeps the Tier 1–3 response ladder of Part 29.3 and the rule that Haiti's own treatment of foreigners is not a mirror of abuses against Haitians (29.5).

### 45.1 The numbers that define the relationship

- **Dominican exports to Haiti, January–May 2026: US$563.1 million (up 24%).** Haitian exports to the Dominican Republic over the same period: **US$0.35 million (down 91.5%).** Source: Dominican customs data as reported in the Dominican press. At that pace Dominican sales to Haiti exceed US$1.3 billion a year (my extrapolation). The ratio is roughly 1,600 to 1.
- Earlier: US$775.8 million in January–August 2025, while the official land border was closed to everything except a corridor for essentials, mainly food.
- What moves: Dominican **free-zone** exports to Haiti are mostly textiles and plastics; **national-regime** exports include steel bars, cement and wheat flour. Haitian exports to the DR are mainly rags, rope and cord, shirts, ethyl alcohol, and pesticides.
- **Deportations:** more than 600,000 people returned from the DR since late 2024 by Dominican counts (Part 29).
- **What this means:** Haiti is a very large customer for the Dominican Republic and the Dominican economy has a real stake in a calm border. That is Haiti's leverage. It is also Haiti's vulnerability: Haitian families depend on the corridor for food, cement and steel, and a trade war would hurt Haitian consumers first.

### 45.2 The Gate: very strict control of goods means customs that cannot be bought

Strictness is a property of customs *integrity*, not of tariff height. The IMF's 2025 governance diagnostic says tax and customs administrations remain exposed to arbitrary action and corruption. A 50% tariff collected by a customs service that can be bribed produces smuggling and rich inspectors, not revenue.

1. **Random inspection assignment.** An algorithm, not an officer, selects which container or truck is opened and which inspector opens it. Assignments are logged and cannot be changed by the shift supervisor.
2. **Scanners and weighbridges** at the four Gates (Part 29.1), with images saved and sampled for audit.
3. **One window.** Every declaration is filed once, electronically, and posted on the ledger (importer, value, origin, quantity, tax paid). Declared values are checked against a price database.
4. **Lot traceability.** Imported food carries a lot code from the exporter to the Haitian shelf, so a contaminated or mislabeled lot can be pulled.
5. **License and register.** Importers and customs brokers are licensed and listed with their beneficial owners (Part 46). A **market-concentration report** is published each year, because a few importing families controlling a category is a form of capture (Art. 250 of the 1987 Constitution forbids a monopoly granted to a private individual).
6. **Rotation and pay.** Inspectors rotate Gates; a pooled performance bonus is tied to revenue *and* audit results; lifestyle audits compare declared assets with observed spending (Part 39.5).
7. **The small trader.** Thousands of Haitian women traders (*madan sara*) and small border merchants live off cross-border trade. A **simplified micro-trade regime** (small value ceiling, one card, fast lane, fixed small fee) keeps them in the legal economy; strict rules aimed at big shipments must not turn the poorest into criminals.
8. **The Line** is guarded by the army (Part 44.3) to stop smuggling routes between Gates.

### 45.3 Product standards: organic and safe, for everyone — honestly

**The request:** any product the Dominican Republic sells to Haiti must be organic-certified, and Haiti must be able to sell the same goods to the DR if it wants.

**What can and cannot be done, as designed:**

- **A rule aimed only at Dominican goods would be discriminatory.** It would breach the most-favored-nation and national-treatment rules of the World Trade Organization (both Haiti and the DR are members), and the standards agreements require measures to be based on science and applied without discrimination between suppliers. It would also hand the DR an easy legal complaint. The same applies to the existing 50% tariff on Dominican goods (Part 29.2), which I flag again as a legal risk.
- **A non-discriminatory standard is allowed.** The same rule can apply to goods from every country *and* to Haitian producers. Origin-neutral rules still protect the Haitian consumer and the Haitian farmer.
- **"Organic for everything" would hurt the poor.** Nearly 6 million Haitians face acute food insecurity. Most imported staples (rice, flour, sugar, oil) do not have organic equivalents on the scale Haiti needs. A blanket organic rule would remove supply and raise prices.

**The design:**

1. **Tier 1 — Safety and labeling for every food and agricultural import, from every country:** an approved-certificate system for pesticide residues, pathogens, and additives, enforced by sampling at the Gate and by lot traceability. Mandatory origin labels.
2. **Tier 2 — Organic certification required for named categories** where Haitian organic production or regional supply exists, or can be built within a stated period (fresh vegetables, fruit, eggs, poultry products are the candidates to test first). Each category has a **published sufficiency test** (the one Art. 251 of the 1987 Constitution needs: food that is "produced in sufficient quantity in the national territory" may not be imported except in force majeure) and a **published start date**, with an exemption only through a public, time-limited shortage window decided by the sufficiency index, not by a minister's signature.
3. **Tier 3 — Conformity certificates for non-food goods** (cement, steel, textiles, plastics): quality, safety and chemical standards, again for every origin.
4. **A voluntary organic label** for any product, so shoppers can pay for the standard they want.
5. **Build the Haitian side at the same time:** cheap certification, extension services, seeds and organic inputs, cold chain and market access (Part 28). Without this, a standard simply moves money to importers.
6. **Reciprocity ("mirror access").** For every product category the DR is allowed to sell under these standards, Haiti seeks the same access for the same Haitian category. If a category of Haitian goods that meets the standard is blocked in the DR, Haiti asks the DR's government, the CARICOM–DR trade framework and the WTO to resolve it, and the Executive Council may restrict the corresponding Dominican category **only through the Tier 1–3 ladder** (Part 29.3), with the beneficiary of the measure disclosed (45.5). Haiti's strongest opening position: *we apply the same standards to everyone, and we want the same access we give.*
7. **Legal homework before launch:** a published legal opinion on WTO and CARICOM–DR compatibility for each measure. Safeguard measures against import surges, anti-dumping and countervailing procedures, and standards regulation are the lawful tools; unilateral discriminatory tariffs are the last resort and carry the most risk.

### 45.4 "Never disrespect a Haitian": the cost-of-disrespect doctrine

**The principle.** Every Haitian, in the Dominican Republic or anywhere, has a state behind them. The response is **never violent, always lawful, always documented and always costly** for the person or institution responsible. Haiti's strength here is evidence, law, diaspora, diplomacy and trade — not force. The Dessalines Charter's Art. 36 (never to conquer or disturb neighbors) and Art. 25–26 (friendly foreigners are protected) are the other half: Haiti is firm against abuse, courteous to honest partners.

**What can be taken from the Israeli example, and what cannot.** The part that is transferable: a state that visibly stands behind its citizens abroad, a well-organized diaspora, standing legal and advocacy capacity, and steady diplomacy that treats mistreatment as a cost the other side will bear. The part that is not: Israel's military and alliance weight. Haiti's leverage is smaller and different: documented evidence, a diaspora with economic and political weight in North America, France and Canada, a large market for Dominican exporters, and coalitions in CARICOM, the African Union and the UN. **Credibility is the asset: Haiti publishes only what it can prove, so no one can say it cries wolf.**

**The service.** A **Haitian Rights Defense Service** (under Foreign Affairs and the GLDD, Part 5.8):

- A 24-hour hotline (phone, messaging app, USSD), in Kreyòl.
- Consular visits to detention centers and a rapid-response lawyer in the DR and in the main host countries (retainer-based, not volunteer-only).
- A **Case File** for every incident: date, place, witnesses, officer or employer, photos, medical record, outcome. The file is the same evidence used for Tier 1–3 responses (Part 29.3).
- Documentation at source: universal birth registration (Part 36) means a Haitian abroad always has a proof of identity.
- A **Defense Fund**: small voluntary diaspora contributions, a board drawn by lot, audited, spent only on legal defense and victim support, never on campaigns.

**The ladder of costs (cumulative, and every step documented):**

| Level | Measure | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Document the incident; support the victim | Always, for every case |
| 1 | Diplomatic note; consular access; public report | Fast; no political decision needed beyond a protest |
| 2 | **Legal action:** local courts; the Inter-American Commission and Court on Human Rights; UN human rights mechanisms; the ILO for labor abuses | The Inter-American Court has already ruled against the DR in *Expelled Dominicans and Haitians v. Dominican Republic* (judgment of 28 August 2014), finding that the Constitutional Tribunal's 2013 ruling on nationality and the 2014 naturalization law violated rights to nationality, due process and non-discrimination, among others. The DR's acceptance of the Court's authority has been contested; check the current state before relying on it |
| 3 | **Named-person consequences:** entry bans by Haiti on abusers; evidence packages to third countries that have their own human-rights sanctions laws (for example, the US, Canada and the EU have such regimes) so that *the responsible individuals* — not a whole nation — face visa bans and asset measures | Haiti cannot impose these on other countries' behalf; it supplies evidence and asks |
| 4 | **The commercial ladder** of Part 29.3 (tariff escalation, category suspension, Tier-3 suspension) | Needs independent corroboration at Tier 3 |
| 5 | **Company-level pressure:** a published "Respect Index" of how states and companies treat Haitian workers and visitors; complaints to buyers' and investors' compliance units; labor-standard certification (Part 29.2) | Publish, do not boycott; keep the state out of calls for private boycotts |

**Anywhere in the world.** The same service and ladder cover Haitians in the Bahamas, the Turks and Caicos, Chile, Brazil, Mexico, Canada, France, the United States and elsewhere. An annual Respect Index informs travel advisories and bilateral diplomacy.

### 45.5 Guardrails: the doctrine must not become a tool for someone's profit

Trade measures create winners. A suspension of Dominican cement favors someone who imports cement from elsewhere. Rules:

1. **No single official triggers any measure above Level 2.** The Executive Council acts (Tier 1–2) with Assembly ratification, or the Assembly acts on a two-thirds vote with independent corroboration (Tier 3).
2. **Beneficiary disclosure.** Any trade restriction published with the list of importers and producers who gain and lose from it.
3. **Conflict recusal.** Any covered person (Part 39) with an interest in a category subject to a restriction is recused and must say so.
4. **Time limits and automatic expiry** unless renewed with new evidence.
5. **Reverse test.** A measure that makes consumers worse off without increasing the evidence-based pressure on abusers is reviewed after 30 days.

### 45.6 Honest limits

- The corridor of food and building materials is a lifeline for Haitians. Hard measures must be weighed against that.
- Legal action takes years; the 2014 judgment is more than a decade old.
- The Dominican government may ignore rulings. The point of the doctrine is cumulative cost, credibility, and allies — including Dominican exporters, churches and civil-society groups who do not want a conflict — not instant results.
- Everything in 45.3 needs a published legal opinion before it is launched.

## PART 46 — THE "BULLET-PROOF" REVIEW: CORRUPTION, PRIVATE-SECTOR CAPTURE AND FOREIGN INFLUENCE

### 46.1 What "bullet-proof" can honestly mean

No institutional design is bullet-proof. What can be built is a system where **every office has a check that is a different body, selected a different way, on a different timeline**, where corruption is **detectable, expensive and unprofitable**, and where the weak points are written down so they can be watched. This review applies that test to the whole document, adds the missing controls, and lists what remains uncovered.

**The test, for every office, rule and flow of money:** *If this were captured or abused, what stops it — and is that stopping mechanism a different body, chosen differently, on a different timeline from the thing it is checking?*

### 46.2 Controls added by this review

**A. Beneficial-owner register.** Every company bidding for a public contract, concession, license, import permit or hub support — and every owner of rural land above a set size — discloses the natural persons who own or control it (10% or more, or any stake held by a covered person or relative). Public on the ledger. Bearer shares are banned; nominee shareholders must be disclosed; a false filing is a crime and a ground for debarment. This is the foundation for the giver's debarment (Part 39.6), the straw-man ban on foreign land (Part 38.4) and the importer concentration report (Part 45.2).

**B. Concessions and strategic assets (mines, quarries, ports, airports, spectrum, power plants, large land).** The 1987 Constitution makes mines, quarries, springs, rivers and coasts public domain and gives the State and the landowner an equal share of profits (Art. 36-5, 36-6). Rules:

1. Open public tender only, no direct award; conflicts and beneficial owners disclosed (A).
2. **The full contract, with annexes and financial model, is published within seven days of signing;** narrow carve-outs only (the same ones as Part 8).
3. Two-thirds Assembly approval **and** a **binding** review by the Superior Court of Auditors (CSCCA) before signing. Today Art. 200-4 makes the Court's review of draft state contracts consultative; this makes it binding above a set value.
4. A fixed term with a sunset; no sale or transfer of the concession without fresh approval; an environmental bond; the statutory profit-share to the landowner and the State; revenue paid into the sovereign account posted on the ledger.
5. Disputes under Haitian law in Haitian courts, or a regional arbitration forum approved by the Assembly with public hearings. No secret international arbitration clause.
6. Every official in the chain is a covered person with the cooling-off periods of Part 39.9.

**C. A Competition Authority.** Art. 250 forbids a monopoly granted to a private individual. An independent body, with a cross-selected board (Part 2 logic), investigates cartels and dominant positions in imports and distribution, reviews mergers, and publishes the importer-concentration report. Without it, the private sector captures the *market* even when it fails to capture the *official*.

**D. A foreign-influence disclosure regime — narrow on purpose.**

1. **Foreign money is banned from campaigns, parties and referendum campaigns.**
2. **Disclosure, not prohibition,** for organizations (NGOs, media, think tanks, unions, parties) receiving **foreign-government** money above a threshold: the amount and source go on the ledger. No licence, deregistration or criminal penalty for receiving money or for political speech; the only offence is failing to disclose, enforced only in ordinary courts.
3. **Officials' meetings with representatives of foreign governments** are logged like business contacts (Part 39.5).
4. **Gifts, scholarships, visas or jobs offered by a foreign government to officials or their families** are banned if given personally, and disclosed if institutional.
5. **Why it is narrow.** "Foreign agent" laws elsewhere have been used to silence critics and independent groups. This one covers money from governments only (not private individuals or the diaspora), never criminalizes receiving it, and sunsets for review every three years.

**E. Political finance for the 2026–27 cycle and after.** Contribution caps, names of donors published within 48 hours, a ban on gifts from firms with matters before the state, spending limits, and the Day-0 shield for every elected official (Part 39.11).

**F. The permanent civil service: where corruption migrates.** The six-month Council and the two-year Assembly rotate; the permanent officials do not. Attackers will go where the power stays. Rules for customs, tax, procurement, licensing, courts' clerks and regulators: **random case and inspection assignment, rotation of posts, lifestyle audits (declared assets compared with observed spending), a ban on outside paid work in the regulated sector, a two-year cooling-off before joining a firm they regulated, written reasons for every discretionary decision posted to the ledger, and integrity tests** (Part 39.7).

**G. Information.** Media ownership transparency (Part 6); state advertising allocated by a public formula, not by favor; the Haiti Data Desk (Part 40.8) answers falsehoods with evidence. No state power over what is said (Parts 6, 40.7).

### 46.5 What no design in this document can fix

1. **A captured judiciary.** If courts can be bought or frightened, debarment, disgorgement and prosecution are empty threats.
2. **Physical intimidation.** Armed groups and their backers can threaten officials, judges, inspectors and witnesses. Protection and the security effort come first (Part 18.4).
3. **Money.** There is no cost model for this document, and the state's budget is small.
4. **The legal route.** The 1987 amendment rules (Arts. 282–284) make constitutional change slow, and forbid a referendum as an amendment method.
5. **Economic dependency.** Remittances, imports of food and fuel, and apparel exports create exposure no governance design removes (Part 9.2).
6. **Disinformation.** The state can answer; it must not censor.
7. **Unknown unknowns.** Every system looks stronger on paper than in the field.

**The recommended next step:** a published **adversarial review** by a mixed panel of Haitian jurists, auditors, police and prison officers, farmers, merchants and diaspora specialists, plus international anti-corruption practitioners paid by Haiti (so they are not a foreign voice in the process), with an open call for people to try to break the design. Results published, changes tracked on the ledger.

## PART 47 — PUBLIC VOICE: HOW ANYONE CAN COMMENT, VOTE AND CHANGE THIS DOCUMENT

This document is written for the people it governs. Part 18.2 says a constitution without broad ownership fails at ratification; this Part builds the ownership in from the start.

### 47.1 What anyone can do

- **Comment on any section**, in any language (Kreyòl, French, English, Spanish, Portuguese, or another), and propose wording, a change, a deletion or an addition.
- **Vote on any section:** *approve*, *approve with changes*, or *disapprove*. One vote per person per section; a person may change their vote.
- **Say who they are:** name, country and commune of residence, occupation, how long they have lived there, and whether they live in Ayiti or abroad. This is so the reviewers can see **which communities support or reject each section** (for example, farmers against merchants, residents against the diaspora), not to give some voices more weight than others.
- **Optionally** leave an email, with a separate consent, to be contacted about the review.

### 47.2 What a vote means and does not mean

- A section vote is an **opinion signal**, not a referendum. It is open, unverified and can be organized by a group. It is used to find disagreement and to prioritize revisions. It does not adopt or reject anything.
- The binding decisions follow the legal route (Part 38.3) and the Constituent process (Part 18.3).
- **No weighting by identity.** Everyone's vote counts once. Breakdowns by place and occupation are published to *show* who thinks what, with the sample size beside each figure.

### 47.3 What happens to the comments

1. Comments are read in the original language and translated by a human reviewer where needed.
2. Each section keeps a **public revision log**: what was proposed (in summary), what was changed, and what was not and why. Nothing disappears without a reason in the log.
3. A suggestion that would weaken an anti-capture rule (Part 19.1, Part 39) is logged and flagged for the adversarial review (Part 46.5), because attempts to weaken those rules are the pattern the rules exist to stop.
4. Revisions are published as new document versions with a visible list of changes.

### 47.4 Protecting the people who speak

Giving a political opinion under a real name can be dangerous in a country where armed groups and powerful interests threaten critics, and some visitors live abroad under other pressures.

- **Names, emails, comments and exact locations are never published.** Only totals and breakdowns with a minimum group size (for example, no breakdown shown if fewer than 20 people fall in a group) are shown publicly.
- **Location is stored only to the commune or city level,** not a street address.
- **Email is kept in a separate table** from comments and votes, used only for the review, never sold, shared or used for campaigning.
- **Access is limited** to a small named review team, with every access logged. Data are stored securely, are deleted on request, and are deleted after the review period unless the person asks otherwise.
- **A person may say they prefer not to be named in any quote.** Quotes in the revision log are anonymized.
- **A security incident is announced to the people affected.**

### 47.5 Preventing manipulation

- **One vote per person per section,** enforced by email where given and otherwise by technical limits.
- **Rate limits and spam checks;** duplicate and copy-pasted submissions are grouped and counted once.
- **Flagged campaigns:** if many near-identical submissions arrive in a short time, the log says so, and they are shown as one organized position next to the independent responses.
- **Foreign-organized campaigns** are treated like any other organized campaign: shown separately, never banned for coming from outside, never counted as more or less.

### 47.6 Honest limits

- It cannot prove that a submitter is who they say they are. The profile data are self-declared.
- People with no phone, data plan or electricity cannot take part online. The Sovereign Houses (Part 3) and the radio-and-SMS channel (USSD, as in Part 8) are the planned route, and a paper-and-voice form read by trained facilitators is needed for the rest.
- A site that collects sensitive opinions is itself a target; security is a standing cost, not a one-time task.

## SUMMARY: WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

This document is designed to be handed to a legislative drafting body, an economist, or another AI system to continue refining — it is a structural skeleton, not a finished constitution. The next honest step for any part of it is the same question asked throughout: *if this specific office or rule were captured or abused, what stops it — and is that stopping mechanism a different body, selected a different way, on a different timeline, than the thing it's checking?* Wherever the answer is "nothing" or "the same people," that section needs another pass before implementation.

**What the October 2026 revision (Parts 37–47) changed in the overall picture:** the system now has a symbolic root (the 1805 Charter), an honest legal route (Part 38: ordinary law now, advisory citizen panels next, strict constitutional change last), a defense for the period between selection and swearing-in (Part 39), a child-protection framework that regulates fundraising appeals without regulating speech (Part 40), a judgment-first path out of armed groups (Part 41), prisons and a technology hub that give people a trade (Parts 42–43), a public force that no foreign party commands or pays (Part 44), a strict, origin-neutral trade gate plus a lawful cost for disrespect of Haitians anywhere (Part 45), a review that lists what cannot be fixed by design (Part 46), and a way for anyone to comment and vote on every section (Part 47).
